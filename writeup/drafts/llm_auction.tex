\begin{abstract}
This paper investigates the behavior of simulated AI agents (large language models, or LLMs) in auctions, introducing a novel synthetic data-generating process to help facilitate the study and design of auctions. We find that LLMs -- when endowed with chain of thought reasoning capacity -- agree with the experimental literature in auctions across a variety of classic auction formats. In particular, we find that LLM bidders produce results consistent with risk-averse human bidders; that they perform closer to theoretical predictions in obviously strategy-proof auctions; and, that they succumb to the winner's curse in common value settings. On prompting, we find that LLMs are not very sensitive to naive changes in prompts (e.g., language, currency) but can improve dramatically towards theoretical predictions with the right mental model (i.e., the language of Nash deviations). We run 1,000$+$ auctions for less than $\$$400 with GPT-4 models (three orders of magnitude cheaper than modern auction experiments) and develop a framework flexible enough to run auction experiments with any LLM model and a wide range of auction design specifications, facilitating further experimental study by decreasing costs and serving as a proof-of-concept for the use of LLM proxies. 
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:introduction}

Rapidly advancing capabilities of large language models (LLMs) raise an exciting possibility: might LLM agents serve as cost-effective proxies for human bidders, thereby making experiments orders of magnitude cheaper to run? In this paper, we contribute to an emerging literature on LLM proxies by testing whether LLM agents can replicate key empirical regularities of the auctions literature. We map LLM behavior in standard auction formats against established human and theoretical benchmarks from economics, providing a proof-of-concept for low-cost, large-scale auction experimentation using LLM bidders.

We begin by benchmarking play with LLM bidders in classic environments: sealed-bid auctions in independent, private-value settings. When there are departures from the theory, we are interested in whether the departures agree with empirical results \citep{kagel1993independent, kagel2020handbook}. We find that agents fail to replicate revenue equivalence results (as existing empirical evidence suggests). We find that bids in the SPSB auction are higher than bids under the FPSB auction, as would be expected, but that there is a smaller separation between the two than predicted by theory. This is primarily due to bids under the FPSB auction being higher than the risk-neutral Bayes-Nash equilibrium suggests. One possible explanation is that LLMs play according to some level of risk aversion: this is consistent with \citet{cox1988theory}'s survey of over 1,500 IPV auction experiments, which finds that revenue under the FPSB auction is higher than under the SPSB auction and suggests that this is due to risk aversion. In addition, we see LLMs in the SPSB auction tend to submit bids lower than their value to an extent that is not echoed in the experimental economics literature. We also supplement this analysis with simulations of the third-price sealed bid (TPSB) and all-pay auctions.

Next, we fix the second-price auction and consider classic ways to make the auction `easier' or `harder' to play, to learn whether LLMs exhibit behavior similar to humans in the face of cognitive constraints. 

Theoretical and experimental studies~\citep{li2017obviously} argue that ascending clock formats are less cognitively demanding than sealed-bid ones, causing humans to make less mistakes relative to the dominant strategy of bidding one's value. We find that LLMs also find clock formats easier to play -- they are more likely to exit the auction (the analogue to bidding in the sealed-bid format) at their value. Following \citet{li2017obviously}, we study this in an affiliated private value setting, replicating their results with LLMs. We also compare the typical clock auction with a blind variant (a clock auction where bidders do not know when others drop out) and the corresponding second-price sealed-bid auction, and find that switching to a clock format accounts for most of the improvement in LLM play. For robustness, we also run these experiments in an IPV setting and obtain the same results.


Next, we add complexity to the auction environment by considering the second-price auction under a common value setting. Under common values, all agents agree on their value for the prize $c$, but each only observes their valuation for the prize under some idiosyncratic, private measurement error $c + p_i$. This auction environment is more cognitively demanding than the independent, private values case as agents do not know whether their observed high valuations are due to high draws of $c$ or the measurement error $p_i$, and this tension produces the winner's curse: that the winning agents wish they had never won, having been adversely selected to be the bidder who least appreciated their high signal was due to a high $p_i$. The winner's curse is purely a non-rational phenomenon -- only the cognitively limited agent suffers from it. The existing economics literature finds humans regularly suffer the winner's curse in common value settings, and we find results vindicating this evidence with LLM agents: LLMs succumb to the winner's curse as well. In particular, \citet{kagel1986winner} famously find experimental evidence for the winner's curse and argue that the winner's curse barely bites with 3-4 bidders but seriously bites for larger auctions with 6+ bidders. We find qualitatively similar results hold with LLM agents as well.

These classic results are some of the reasons economists have found auction theory to be beautiful over the last few decades, but auctions can also be useful \citep{milgrom2020clock}. Auctions are used to clear billions of dollars of value annually, and one may ask how LLMs fare in less controlled settings. To answer this, we design a more realistic auction environment inspired by eBay's online marketplace. We examine two design parameters: a hidden reserve price and a modified closing rule (soft-close, which extends the auction when there is bidding activity). First, we demonstrate that LLM agents replicate emergent, real-world bidding behaviors seen on eBay with no prompting: in auctions with a hard closing time, LLMs frequently delay bids until the last second to `snipe' their competitors as humans would. Second, experiments testing the design of an extended closing rule greatly reduce bid sniping and improve price discovery, echoing dynamics of online auctions between Amazon and eBay in the early 2000s \citep{roth2002last}.

The ability to interface more directly with our experimental subjects also enables us to study the explanation of mechanisms more carefully. Auctions are most prized as mechanisms to allocate goods efficiently, but if agents don't understand the rules they can't be expected to play well. How can we describe and explain mechanisms to empower optimal play cheaply? We conclude the body of our paper by testing interventions designed to influence or aid LLM bidders' understanding of the underlying economics of the setting and its willingness to follow rules. In particular, we test six interventions: an extensive-form menu description meant to simplify the equilibrium logic inspired by \citet{gonczarowski2023strategyproofness}; a description describing the auction via a clock (without actually running the clock auction) inspired by \citet{breitmoser2022obviousness}; a description walking an LLM through the logic of Nash deviations; a description which also reveals the correct statement that the SPSB's dominant strategy is to bid one's value; and a description that incorrectly reveals a false statement that the SPSB's dominant strategy is to bid half of one's value. We find that that all the interventions that were meant to improve the correctness of an LLM agent's bidding strategy helped, with the logic of Nash deviations helping the most of the first three. Unsurprisingly, the false statement intervention caused LLMs to play poorly, but interestingly, the LLM continued to experiment with new strategies upon determining the given advice was bad.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. In Section 1.1, we report related work. In Section 2, we describe our simulation procedure and prompting methods in more depth. In Section \ref{section:classic}, we benchmark LLM play against sealed-bid auctions in independent, private value settings. Then, for the rest of the paper, we fix the game as a second-price auction and test various design considerations. In Section \ref{session:OSP}, we vary the auction format and find that LLMs play clock formats the best. In Section \ref{session:winner}, we add cognitive complexity to the auction environment by switching to a common value environment and show that LLMs succumb to the winner's curse. Both of these sections replicate key empirical regularities found in laboratory and real-life play in auctions with humans. In Section~\ref{session:ebay}, we implement auctions inspired by eBay's marketplace and provide new evidence on famous debates on design parameters. In Section~\ref{session:Intervention}, we introduce interventions designed to influence or aid LLM bidders' understanding of the underlying economics of the setting. We find that prompting matters, and that, surprisingly, LLMs improve play dramatically when instructed to follow the logic of Nash deviations.

In the Appendix we report complete simulation procedures, prompts and numerous robustness checks. In particular, we ran experiments with prompts from other contemporaneous auctions experiments (e.g., the Appendix script from \citet{li2017obviously}), with prompts in different languages and with different currencies, and with varying numbers of bidders. All experiments are run with chain-of-thought agents, but we also run an ablation study with out-of-the-box agents. We also find interesting evidence that `goal' prompting (e.g., reminding the LLM that the goal is to maximize profit) leads to bidding that more closely follows rational economic theory, leading to higher allocative efficiency. See also~\citet{manning2024automated} for a related discussion. 

To obtain the data for these empirical results, we have developed a code repository to systematically run experiments 
with a certain number of bidders and a particular prompt. 
In particular, our repository is flexible enough that it can be used to generate synthetic data for almost any describable  auction format and including auctions with single or multiple goods.\footnote{We will make the code-base public  and hope this will facilitate additional research into the role of LLMs as modeling human bidder behavior.}

\subsection{Related Work}




\textbf{Auctions:}
There's a vast quantity of theoretical and experimental literature on auctions. 
While \citet{krishna2009auction}'s textbook and \citet{kagel2020handbook}'s handbook provided invaluable general resources, we will stay focused only on the citations relevant to the results in this paper.

Starting with the IPV case, the benchmark of revenue equivalence in the risk-neutral case is exposited in the seminal 
\citet{myerson1981optimal}. 
The experimental evidence for departures due to risk-aversion in the FPSB auction is thoroughly documented in \citet{coppinger1980incentives} and \citet{cox1988theory}'s survey. 
The experimental evidence for the common error of bidding above one's value in the SPSB auction is documented in \citet{kagel1993independent}.

For common value settings, the winner's curse has been documented empirically and experimentally. 
Experimental evidence for the winner's curse was first given by \citet{bazerman1983won}. 
For the present paper, we primarily follow the empirical approach in \citet{kagel1986winner} (FPSB auctions with varying numbers of bidders) and \citet{kagel1987information} (SPSB auctions with informational interventions). 
Empirically, the winner's curse was first documented in 1971, in auctions for oil drilling rights by \citet{capen1971competitive}. 

Theoretically, while the winner's curse is fundamentally a problem of non-rational play,  \citet{charness2009origin} provide a model to cast the adverse-selection problem as a failure from cognitive constraints. 


Recent work on obvious strategy-proofness began with \citet{li2017obviously}, who demonstrates empirically that human subjects tend to be less truthful in second price sealed bid auctions than ascending clock auctions in the APV setting, even though the two auctions are strategically equivalent. 
Li also provides a theoretical framework for the results. 
To better understand our simulations, we also consider the experimental evidence presented by \citet{breitmoser2022obviousness}, who investigate intermediate auction formats that decompose the behavioral effects in \citet{li2017obviously}. 

\textbf{LLMs as simulated agents:} Recent LLMs, having been trained on an enormous corpus of human-generated data, are able to generate human-like text and reasoning \citep{achiam2023gpt, bubeck2023sparks}. 
Yet, they are far from perfect -- in particular, displaying limited planning abilities and reflecting various cognitive biases endemic to human agents \citep{wan2023kelly}. 

There is a growing literature on using these human-like AI models as simulated agents in economics and social science studies \citep{aher2023using, park2023generative, brand2023using}. 
In this literature, \citet{horton2023large} replicates four classical behavioral economics experiments by endowing a single LLM agent with different personas and querying it about its decisions and preferences.
\citet{manning2024automated} enable multiple GPT-4 agents to interact and simulate various social science scenarios, including bargaining, job interviews, and bail-setting.
Finally, \citet{raman2024steerassessingeconomicrationality} benchmark the ability of LLM agents to conduct rational play over a broad range of tasks.

Compared to humans, the inference cost of LLM queries is very low and continues to decrease \citep{achiam2023gpt,patel2023splitwise,bae2023complexitynet}.

Beyond acting as decision-makers, LLMs can also prepare the very environments in which simulated agents operate.
Recent work shows that LLMs can parse natural-language narratives (e.g., novels, news articles, or other free form texts) and translate them into formal game-theoretic settings, thereby generating the strategic context required for agent-based experiments \citep{daskalakis2024charting, mensfelt2024autoformalization, deng2025natural}.
In parallel, LLMs are being used as preference-elicitation tools that help existing economic mechanisms gather richer, textual inputs from humans \citep{soumalias2025llm, huang2025accelerated}.


\textbf{LLMs in auctions:}
There are some works that use LLMs as simulated agents in auction experiments.
\citet{fish2024algorithmic} study the collusion behaviors in first-price sealed-bid auction of two LLM agents
 under the context of LLMs as a price setter for companies. 
\citet{chen2023put} study how to make an LLM better at playing auctions than humans.
Finally, \citet{manning2024automated} run a more limited study an a variant of an open-ascending clock auction with three bidders, focusing on deviations from rational economic theory in considering bidders' values and the final clearing price.

% focusing on deviations from rational economic theory in considering bidders' values and

\textbf{Automated Mechanism Design:} Pioneering work by \citet{conitzer2002complexity, conitzer2004self} introduced \emph{automated mechanism design} (AMD), which uses computational search to design optimal, incentive-compatible auctions \citep{curry102automated}.
A newer line -- often labeled “differentiable economics’’ \citep{dutting2024optimal} -- pushes this idea further with deep learning, producing optimal auctions \citep{dutting2024optimal}, combinatorial auctions \citep{wang2025bundleflow}, budget-constrained bidders \citep{feng2018deep}, payment minimization \citep{tacchetti2019neural}, two-sided matching \citep{ravindranath2021deep}, and data-market pricing \citep{ravindranathdata}.
Structural advances such as \citet{wang2024gemnet} obtain \emph{exact} strategy-proofness rather than approximate incentive compatibility. Whereas automated-mechanism-design (AMD) research pushes the analytical frontier of optimal auction design, our study advances the behavioral understanding of how LLM-based agents act within classical formats. 


\section{Methods}

\subsection{LLM as Auction Participants}

We use a single LLM API call to represent one bidder in our auction simulations. Unlike traditional agent-based modeling, we do not anchor bidder behaviors or personas mechanically beyond a name to index them (e.g., Alice, Betty or Charles). Instead, we allow LLMs to conduct their own strategic reasoning and in-context learning to generate bids. Hence, we focus on how these LLM agents strategically interact, without concern for demographic representativeness, as our primary interest is rational and strategic behavior in the mechanism.

To ensure that LLM agents make profit-maximizing decisions, we append a prompt prefix that explicitly instructs them to seek long-run profit maximization. This instruction is universal in all the experiments ran in this paper, and is inspired by \citep{fish2024algorithmic, chen2023put}. 
Complete details on prompts and execution can be found in Appendix ~\ref{app:process}. Specific implementation details for non-sealed-bid formats (the clock and eBay auction formats) are provided in their respective sections.

Before eliciting bids in each period, we provide a description of the auction rules along with randomly drawn values and information from previous rounds. Standard prompting techniques like chain-of-thought \citep{wei2022chain} are also used to mimic the strategic thinking period in auction practices \citep{li2017obviously}.

\subsection{Simulation Procedure}\label{sec:process}

For each auction experiment, we simulate $n$ (often 3) LLM agents bidding against one another. Each setting is repeated multiple times, with randomized private values drawn for each round. We design our multi-round protocol to mirror standard laboratory experiments in the auction literature. The item to be bid in standard auctions is specified as a ``prize''. In eBay auctions, we arbitrarily set the prize item as a ``256GB IPhone 16 pro'' and the item condition is set to be ``used'' for realism.

Below is our simulation procedure for each auction; full prompt texts are in Appendix~\ref{app:prompt}, and an example round is in Appendix~\ref{app:process}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item {\textit{Planning.} LLM agents receive a description of the auction format and are asked to propose a bidding plan.}
    \item {\textit{Value Realization.} Each LLM agent is assigned a random private value.}
    \item {\textit{Bidding.} Given their value, each agent places a bid according to their stated plan.}
    \item {\textit{Outcome.} The system computes allocation and payments. Each LLM agent learns its own profit or loss outcome, as well as the full set of bids submitted (but not other agents' private values).}
    \item {\textit{Reflection.} Before the next round, each LLM agent reviews the results and possibly revises its approach. These updates are stored in the \texttt{HISTORY} variable.}
    \item {\textit{Repetition.} One complete “plan-bid-reflect” loop is one round. We repeat 15 rounds in each experiment, with the entire \texttt{HISTORY} carried forward.}
\end{enumerate}

API calls are made using the Python package Expected Parrot Domain-Specific Language (EDSL), a wrapper that structures LLM queries and responses in the desired format \citep{Horton2024EDSL}. Each agent in our sample is supported by a separate API call via EDSL, thereby nullifying the risk of collusion. For every auction, we use models from OpenAI at a temperature of $0.5$.\footnote{{We set a non-zero temperature to encourage diversity in strategies and reflections. No parameter tuning was performed to achieve these results.}} 
The full code base is made open-source for research and replication at \href{https://github.com/KeHang-Zhu/llm-auction}{GitHub}.

\subsection{Discrete Bids and Continuous-Theory Benchmarks}

A fundamental tension in experimental auctions work lies between the use of continuous theory and the necessarily discrete nature of implementation. Auction theory traditionally models bids and valuations as continuous variables, enabling the use of calculus to derive clean analytical results. Yet  real-world auctions -- whether in experimental laboratories or practical markets -- are invariably discrete, with quantized valuations (e.g., integer values from 0 to 99) and finite bid increments (e.g., \$1). This discrete-continuous gap has received surprisingly limited direct attention in the literature, raising the critical question of how to justify the use of continuous-theory benchmarks for our discrete simulations. 

The foundational literature in both theoretical and experimental economics provides a basis for this practice. A deep and influential body of work operates on the principle that, with reasonably fine increments, discrete auctions serve as a good approximation for the continuous counterparts. In many parts of the literature, this intuition is almost implicit (e.g., as in footnote 3 of \cite{noussair2006behavior}), but it has also been formalized in other works, as in \cite{cai2010note}'s convergence results that outcomes in discrete first-price auctions converge to the continuous-case equilibrium as bid increments shrink. This theoretical backing underpins the standard methodology in experimental economics, where pioneers like \cite{kagel1993independent} have long benchmarked behavior in discrete laboratory settings against the predictions of continuous theory.

However, a more modern line of inquiry reveals that convergence arguments do not generalize as smoothly as once hoped, and that model equilibria can be sensitive to environment parameters (e.g., tie-breaking rules, number of bidders, and auction type). This is all the more striking given foundational results like \cite{athey2001single} provide quite general conditions -- most notably, single crossing -- that guarantee the existence of a pure-strategy equilibrium. Yet, even when such equilibria exist, their specific \textit{form} may be fragile. Building on early work from \cite{maskin1985revenue}, recent ``discontinuity'' results from \cite{rasooly2020importance} show that familiar, symmetric pure-strategy equilibria often vanish when bidder valuations and bids are restricted to a discrete grid, yielding only more complex asymmetric or mixed-strategy equilibria. This fragility is particularly acute for the all-pay auction, where Rasooly \& Gavidia-Calderon show that a symmetric, pure-strategy equilibrium fails to exist for a grid of values and bids (no matter how fine), regardless of the tie-breaking rule. This unconditional non-existence is stronger than for the first-price auction, where pure, symmetric equilibria can still exist for a low number of bidders and an even number of possible values. And, this fragility is not just destructive; an example from \cite{boudreau2011all} shows that in some contests, discreteness can be constructive, creating pure-strategy equilibria where none existed in the continuous model.

This leaves the relationship between discrete and continuous auction models as a live and important frontier of research. If we are to continue leveraging the powerful insights from classic auction theory, we must better understand the bridge between discrete and continuous models. The methods of this paper, insofar as they dramatically reduce the cost of obtaining synthetic evidence on auctions, enables progress towards that goal. In particular, future work can feasibly investigate how outcomes change as we vary parameters that theory tells us are operative -- the number of bidders, the granularity of the bid grid, and the specific auction format -- in a way that is currently prohibitively expensive with human subjects.\footnote{In particular, our codebase on GitHub implements a parameter of minimal increment to allow researchers to explore the effect of bidding increments on auctions.} This computational advantage could, in future work, help identify the boundary conditions where continuous models remain valid and reveal strategic phenomena unique to discrete settings.

\section{Benchmarking Against Existing Auctions}\label{results}


\subsection{Sealed Bid Auctions with Independent Private Values}\label{section:classic}
We begin by examining the First-Price Sealed-Bid (FPSB), Second-Price Sealed-Bid (SPSB), Third-Price Sealed-Bid (TPSB) and All-Pay Sealed-Bid auctions in an independent private values (IPV) setting. 


\subsubsection{Setting}

There are three bidders in each auction, and each bidder $i$'s value is drawn from an independent, uniform distribution $v_i \sim U[0, 99]$. Bidders, upon observing their value, submit a sealed bid $\beta(v)$, with $\beta(\cdot)$ a vector mapping each component value to its corresponding bid, corresponding to a strategy profile for reporting based on the valuation profile. 

In the FPSB auction, the highest bidder pays her bid and receives the prize (and all other bidders pay $0$ and receive no prize). Formally, the payment for an agent is given by: $$t_i(\beta(v)) = \mathbbm{1}_{i \; \text{won the auction}}\cdot \beta_i(v).$$
In the SPSB auction, the highest bidder pays the second-highest bid and receives the prize (and all other bidders pay $0$ and receive no prize). 
The payment for an agent is given by: $$t_i(\beta(v)) = \mathbbm{1}_{i \; \text{won the auction}} \cdot \beta^{(2)}(v),$$ where $\beta^{(2)}$ represents the second-order statistic or the second-largest bid. 
Analogously, in the TPSB auction, the highest bidder pays the third-highest bid and receives the prize (and all other bidders pay $0$ and receive no prize). The payment for an agent is given by: $$t_i(\beta(v)) = \mathbbm{1}_{i \; \text{won the auction}} \cdot \beta^{(3)}(v),$$ where $\beta^{(3)}$ represents the third-order statistic or the third-largest bid.
Finally, in the all-pay auction, all bidders pay their bid but only the highest bidder wins the prize. The payment for an agent in the all-pay auction is given by: $$t_i(\beta(v)) = \beta_i(v).$$
Bids are submitted in $\$1$ increments and ties are resolved randomly.

\subsubsection{Empirical benchmarks}

Results from laboratory experiments offer additional empirical benchmarks against which we can compare LLM performance. In general, empirical evidence suggests
monotone bidding strategies\cite{kagel1993independent}, \cite{kagel2020handbook}. This means that higher
valuations consistently lead agents to report higher bids. However, the specific bidding patterns
observed in experiments often deviate from theoretical equilibria in interesting ways.
\paragraph{Empirical findings for FPSB:} In the case of FPSB auctions, experimental evidence consistently
shows bids above the risk-neutral BNE prediction \cite{cox1988theory}. They identify this with bidder
risk-aversion. In an FPSB auction, bidding higher increases the probability of winning but decreases
the potential profit if one does win. Risk-averse bidders may be willing to accept lower potential
profits in exchange for a higher chance of winning, leading to bids above the risk-neutral BNE.
\paragraph{Empirical findings for SPSB:} Similarly, experimental data for SPSB auctions also reveals
evidence of overbidding.  \cite{kagel1987information} and \cite{kagel1993independent} provide insights into this
behavior, in particular highlighting that a significant proportion of participants (typically around
60-70\%) in SPSB auctions submitted bids above their true values. However, in this case, the SPSB
has an equilibrium in dominant strategies, so the explanation is not risk-aversion. These findings
highlight the complexity of human behavior in auction settings. Several explanations have been
proposed, including misconceptions about the second-price rule [\cite{kagel1987information}] and strategic
uncertainty or beliefs about other bidders’ irrationality [\cite{crawford2007level}
].
\paragraph{Empirical findings for TPSB:} There is less empirical evidence for the TPSB. \cite{kagel1987information} test TPSBs in the laboratory setting and found that the empirically observed bidding strategy
was higher than both that of the SPSB and FPSB auctions, as theory predicts. In particular, 90% of
bids exceeded their value in this auction, and risk-aversion induces lower bidding (as opposed to
higher bidding in the case of the FPSB auction).
\paragraph{Empirical findings for all-pay auctions:} There is a plethora of evidence for all-pay auctions
or similar formats [\cite{dechenaux2015survey}]. As in previous formats, one major feature of bidding
behavior is overbidding relative to the BNE prediction: this holds in asymmetric and symmetric
auction environments and with various levels of information. Second, risk-aversion appears to push
bidding downwards (but still above the predicted level). Some experiments also report substantial
heterogeneity in bidding strategies, with bidding strategies differing sharply based on bidder types
(e.g., \cite{gneezy2006all}).

Table \ref{tab:bidding_errors} summarizes the frequency of underbidding, value bidding, and overbidding by LLM bidders, comparing these results directly with human data from \citet{kagel1993independent}. In this section, we've highlighted several features of individual and aggregate LLM bidding behavior. Perhaps the feature of LLM data that makes it the hardest to interpret individual bids is the increased variance. We suggest this increased variance in LLM bidding is due to increased random play by LLMs, due both to a desire to gather exploratory data as well as to disrupt opponents' predictions. The ability to peer into LLMs' chain-of-thought reasoning makes this particularly transparent. For instance, LLM bidders frequently articulate plans like the following from Bidder betty in a round of the FPSB auction: 


\begin{quote}
    "I'll adopt a balanced strategy, bidding $65\%$ of my value. \textbf{I'll also introduce random bids occasionally to disrupt predictability}. Monitoring competitor's bids remains essential to adjust my strategy accordingly."
\end{quote}


This ability to randomize is just one way in which LLMs may differ from human bidders, but there are many others -- say, the ability to reliably calculate 65\% of one's value or to monitor and perform inference over the full history of competitor bids. The variance in LLM play suggests to us that looking at LLM data in aggregate, rather than scrutinizing individual data points, may be more productive. Just as individual human bidders encode their own biases and tendencies into their actions, no individual LLM bidder's behavior is particularly instructive -- however, in aggregate, auction data with LLM data encodes behavioral patterns that may align closely with many of the empirical regularities from auction theory. It is our view that documenting these points of alignment or divergence will make the future use of LLM data for auction theory or mechanism design more productive.

Finally, one may ask how prompting may change the general direction of our results. This is a first-order concern for the eventual use of synthetic data in mechanism design and experimentation. To address this, we pursue two additional lines of inquiry:
%
\begin{itemize}
    \item[(1)] We test prompt robustness. We implement our FPSB and SPSB environments with prompts in a variety of prompt styles, in different languages and with different currencies to study how bids change under prompt variation. These results are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:prompt}. We find that naive changes in prompt style, language or currency do not qualitatively change any of the results reported in this section.
    %
    \item[(2)] Beyond naive prompt changes, we test prompts which influence the LLM agents' understanding of the underlying economics. To do this, we design prompts to teach LLMs particular mental models or avoid particular failure modes; e.g., a prompt to consider Nash deviations, to conceptualize the auction as an extensive-form game, or a prompt which encourages agents to bid in a risk-neutral manner. These results are reported in Section~\ref{session:Intervention}. We find that \textit{these} interventions can dramatically change our results. 
\end{itemize}

Our results suggest to us that prompting \textit{can} induce dramatically different behavior in LLMs by making the underlying logic of mechanisms more apparent, but is unlikely to generate different results with naive changes in prompts. It remains an open engineering question as to `how much' prompting is required to match distributions of existing empirical evidence more closely.

\subsection{Obviously Strategy-proofness}\label{session:OSP}
For the rest of the paper, we restrict our attention to the second-price auction mechanism. Having considered LLM play in a variety of sealed-bid auctions, we now vary the representation of the second-price auction to make the auction `easier' to play. Namely, we turn our attention to clock auctions. Clock auctions are dynamic auction formats where the price increases incrementally (or decreases incrementally in reverse auctions), and bidders decide whether to stay in or drop out at each price point.
% These auctions are strategy-proof mechanisms since a player does not need to form beliefs about other players' values and types; 
% she can simply report her value truthfully (bid her value) because she can do no better regardless of what the other players bid or what their bidding strategies are. While truthful reporting is a weakly dominant strategy here, many people in reality do not report their types truthfully in strategy-proof mechanisms. 
\citet{li2017obviously} provides an argument as to why clock auctions may be easier to recognize as strategy-proof for humans, and formalizes this as an equilibrium refinement on strategy-proofness called {\em obvious strategy-proofness} (OSP).
% A mechanism is OSP if it is evident to a bidder that truthful bidding is optimal, regardless of others' actions, at every decision point.
% OSP mechanisms are simple to play and thus have a lot of desired properties. 


\subsubsection{Setting}
There are three bidders in each auction but now, following \citet{li2017obviously}, bidders draw {\em affiliated private values} (APV) of the form $v_i = c + p_i$. The common component $c$ is drawn once, $c \sim U[0, 20]$, and the private component $p_i$ is drawn $n$ times independently, $p_i \sim U[0, 20]$. Winners of the auction receive their own value of the prize $v_i$ when they win, and the `common' and `private' components  serve to make values correlated. We switch to considering APV settings in this section for two reasons. First, in spirit of our larger goal to provide many benchmarks of LLM play in various environments. Second, to enable comparison with the existing empirical literature; the two largest experiments of which have been conducted in APV settings. 

The {\em ascending clock auction} (AC) has
a global price clock that increases in some fixed increment and bidders who choose when to drop out. The last bidder remaining wins the prize at the price where the second to last bidder dropped out. If multiple bidders are the last to drop out, we resolve ties by randomly allocating the prize among them and setting the winning price to that mutual drop out price. We also study the {\em blind ascending clock auction} (AC-B), a variant 
where bidders are not told when  other bidders   drop out.
%The SPSB auction is as defined in Section \ref{section:classic}. 

\subsubsection{Theoretical benchmarks}
What effect an affiliated values environment has on strategic behavior may not be ex-ante obvious. On one hand, the correlation in values may strengthen incentives for extremal-valued bidders, so that extremal-valued bidders are actually \textit{more} likely to bid their value even in the sealed-bid format (echoing arguments from \citet{li2017obviously}). On the other hand, APV may be cognitively more complex for bidders relative to IPV environments, thereby inducing errors in play. In either case, the three auction formats in this section are strategically equivalent, and so the affiliation is, in the rational sense, a red herring: for all three auctions it is still a dominant strategy to bid (or drop out at) one's value. The SPSB, AC-B and AC auctions are strategically equivalent and share an equilibrium in dominant strategies:
\[{\beta}^{**}(v) = v.\]

An agent's bid, in the ascending clock format, is equivalent to the price at which they drop out of the auction at. We will use the terms `bid' and `drop-out price' interchangeably in the context of clock auctions.
The equilibrium refinement of OSP, introduced by \citet{li2017obviously}, provides a formal framework for analyzing the cognitive simplicity of mechanisms.
Consider a strategy $S_i$ for player $i$. 
We say that $S_i$ {\em obviously dominates} another strategy $S'_i$ if, for any deviation from $S_i$ to $S'_i$, the best possible outcome from $S'_i$ is no better than the worst possible outcome from $S_i$.

Formally, for all histories $h$ where both $S_i$ and $S'_i$ are consistent with $h$:
\begin{equation*}
\min_{z \in Z(S_i, h)} u_i(z) \geq \max_{z' \in Z(S'_i, h)} u_i(z')
\end{equation*}
where $Z(S_i, h)$ is the set of terminal histories that can result from playing $S_i$ after history $h$ and
$u_i(z)$ is player $i$'s utility for terminal history $z$.  
% \dcp{explain in words what a `history` is in a clock auction} 
In a clock auction, the history refers to the sequence of events that track bidder activity throughout the auction. Specifically, it records whether and when bidders drop out as the price increases, providing insight into bidding behavior and competition dynamics. A mechanism is said to be obviously strategy-proof if it has an equilibrium in dominant strategies.  Both ascending clock auctions studied in this paper are obviously strategy-proof, though the AC-B auction provides bidders with less information than the AC auction by suppressing when bidders drop out.


\subsubsection{Empirical benchmarks and results}
For each of the APV auction formats, we perform 5 experiments with 15 rounds and 3 LLM bidders competing over a prize. A single round for the clock format is the run of a global clock from the initial price of $\$0$ to the final clearing price. The complete simulation of a single clock round is provided in \ref{app:dynamic}.

Our main results are plotted in Figure \ref{fig:osp_bid}. We find that the clock formats induce play significantly closer to one's value than the sealed-bid format. Table \ref{tab:auction-comparison} summarizes this concisely: the clock formats, aggregated, have an average difference between bid and value of $1.83$ (with $73.9\%$ of agents dropping out at their value, excluding winners), while the SPSB bidding has an average difference between bid and value of $6.26$ (with $18.7\%$ of all bids equal to an agent's value). This corresponds closely with \citet{li2017obviously}'s experimental results: aggregated across all rounds, he reports a mean difference between bid and value of $2.48$ for the ascending clock auction, and a mean difference of $6.52$ for the sealed-bid auction. In general, the clock format improves play for LLM bidders in much the same way as with human bidders.

\section{Ebay Auction Design}\label{session:ebay}

Having tested LLMs against classic results in auction theory, we  build an auction environment to test components of auction design in a setting inspired by eBay's online marketplace. eBay's auction platform, studied by \citet{einav2011learning} and others, features various design elements -- for example, a hidden reserve price (as opposed to a reserve price)
and proxy bidding -- that are \emph{in theory} less critical to auction outcomes
%(often being revenue-equivalent) given they induce bidders to bid their value,
yet \emph{in practice} appear to have substantial effects on bidder behavior. These differences highlight the gap between textbook theory and real-world bidding, where seemingly small design details can alter bidder behavior and thus price discovery and revenue.

% Having documented encouraging results from surveying LLM agents in classic auction designs, we next explore how LLM agents respond when confronted with more realistic conditions inspired by eBay's online marketplace. 

In this section, we simulate a stylized version of the eBay auction design to consider two design parameters more closely: the hidden reserve price and the implementation of a `closing rule'. The core mechanics of an eBay auction are simple: bidders on eBay use a proxy bidding system, which means that each bidder submits their ``maximum bid" to the platform, and the platform automatically bids on their behalf, only as much as necessary to keep the winning bidder in the lead (i.e., the second-highest bid + \$1, with incremental bidding managed automatically).
Bidders can increase their maximum bid at any point in time, until some closing time of 
the auction, giving this the flavor
of an incremental second-price auction.

% As such, if bidders play in the way eBay expects them to, the hidden reserve and the closing rule shouldn't change bidder strategy -- all bidders report their value as soon as they see the auction, and the auction does not update any further. 

The use of reserve prices in auctions is well understood in auction theory. Notably, 
\citet{myerson1981optimal} showed that setting an optimal reserve price can maximize expected revenue in certain
private value settings (for ``regular" distributions).
However, eBay also allows sellers (for a fee) to set a {\em hidden} reserve price: a threshold that bidders do not know until it is met by the highest maximum bid. 
%
While there is some work suggesting a hidden reserve can increase expected revenue in common value settings (e.g., \citet{vincent1995bidding}), in a private values environment with rational bidders using proxy bidding, the hidden reserve should not change behavior relative to a known reserve or even no reserve: bidders should still submit their maximum value upfront. While it is impossible to say whether bidders are bidding their true values, a large empirical study of hidden reserves on eBay suggests that bidders do not change bidding behavior much in the face of hidden vs.~non-hidden reserves \citep{einav2011learning}. 

A more dramatic departure from simple, truthful bidding is `bid sniping,' or the practice of placing bids in the final moments of an auction to avoid triggering competitive responses.
Sniping is prevalent on eBay, with dedicated e-snipe services automating last-second bid submissions.\footnote{E.g., \url{https://www.esnipe.com/}.} This phenomenon has been studied for decades, with explanations ranging from best responses to irrational types to behavioral inertia \citep{roth2002last}. Such wide prevalence of sniping may be especially troubling to economists, as it inhibits a core function of auctions: their ability to induce timely, efficient price discovery. One avenue to curb sniping may be a `closing rule' (also called a `soft-close' or `auction extension' rule) -- namely, when a new maximum bid comes in at the last minute, extend the auction close to allow for more bidding. This design detail has been credited with the substantial difference in bidding dynamics between earlier auctions on Amazon.com, which had this auction-extension rule, and auctions on eBay, which do not. Specifically, \citet{roth2002last} and \citet{ockenfels2006late} show that much less sniping occured on Amazon.com, with bids on Amazon.com occuring throughout the auction compared to the empirical evidence of sniping on eBay.

%

    \item[T2]: eBay auction with a modified (auction extension) closing rule.
    \item[T3]: Standard eBay auction with a hidden reserve price.
    \item[T4]: eBay with a modified (auction extension) closing rule and a hidden reserve price.
\end{itemize}

For each hidden reserve price design, we run
experiments with hidden reserves at $r = 40, 50$, and $60$.

We model continuous time in the synthetic, modified eBay auction environment

\section{Intervention design in Sealed-Price Auction}\label{session:Intervention}


It matters how agents perceive mechanisms. One seminal example from the market design literature is that of the design of the {\em National Residency Match Program} (NRMP) for doctors. Since the early days of the NRMP, students have mistakenly strategically reported their preferences to the match program despite it being a strategy-proof matching mechanism, thereby hurting the overall efficiency of the system \citep{roth1999redesign, rees2018suboptimal}. Furthermore, improving our understanding of agents' perception of mechanisms may also improve our ability to design them 
to be simple to play~\cite{li2024designing}. 


This section introduces experimental interventions that seek to influence how agents understand a mechanism. We begin with three interventions that prime agents with different conceptual frameworks for determining the equilibrium logic of an auction. 
% \dcp{as per earlier comment, `anchor` is a loaded term in behavioral econ and best avoided}
We then provide two interventions that probe the effect of giving advice. We also provide one behavioral intervention which seeks to affect bidders' risk preferences. 


% An important question for all mechanism design work is the question of how users understand and interface with the mechanism.

 % Designing interventions in a mechanism is challenging. 
 % Even many strategy-proof auction rules fail to make people truthfully bid since showing that a mechanism is strategy-proof requires delicate, formal proofs. 
 % Recent work suggests that different descriptions of a fixed auction rule can influence straightforward play, even transforming a non-OSP implementation into an OSP one.

In the literature, \citet{gonczarowski2023strategyproofness} present a method to highlight and emphasize strategy-proofness with short, one-sentence descriptions in an extensive-form menu\footnote{An extensive-form menu refers to a structured presentation of options or strategies. It outlines the sequential choices available to players, detailing how each decision can lead to subsequent options and outcomes.}. 
% \dcp{footnote to explain `extensive form menu`}
However, their menu-description treatment of a second-price sealed-bid (SPSB) auction did not perform well in the field when they tested it empirically. They argue this may be because the classic descriptions of some mechanisms may be as transparent as the extensive-form menu description.
In contrast, \citet{breitmoser2022obviousness} show that re-framing  a static sealed-bid auction as an ascending clock auction can improve the rate of truth-telling behavior. 
% \dcp{prev sentence doesn't read right. i think you mean something like the oppoisite -- reframing a SPSB as an ascending clock.}
% However, such interventions can be expensive when testing on human participants; for instance, the experiments of \citet{gonczarowski2023strategyproofness} cost around \$2{,}000. \dcp{prev sentence seems out of place. why just say here? move to end of paper?}

Here, we present six interventions on the SPSB auction. Of the six  interventions, the first three seek to prime agents on particular conceptual frameworks,
the next two provide (good and bad) advice, and the last is a behavioral intervention.
%
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Menu-Description (menu\_intervention)}: A treatment providing the extensive form of the auction as a menu to the agent. 
    % \dcp{footnote to briefly explain this}
    %
    \item \textbf{Clock-Description (proxy\_intervention)}: A treatment describing the ascending-clock process. Though the proxy clock is described to the agent before the game begins, the auction is still operated as an SPSB auction.
    %
    \item \textbf{Nash-Deviation (nash\_intervention)}: A treatment providing an example of a Nash-style thought process; e.g., What happens if you bid up? What happens if you bid down?
    %
    \item \textbf{Direct Revelation (dominant\_strat\_intervention)}: A treatment directly revealing the dominant strategy of the auction as that of bidding one's value.
    %
    \item \textbf{Wrong Direct Revelation (wrong\_strat\_intervention)}: An ablation study incorrectly revealing the dominant strategy of the auction as bidding \textit{half} one's value.
    %
    \item \textbf{Risk-neutrality prompting (risk\_intervention)}: A behavioral treatment defining risk-neutrality in terms of gambles and then prompting the LLM to be more risk-neutral.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
The full intervention prompts are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:intervention} and are appended to the end of the rule explanation prompt when eliciting LLM bids.


These interventions aim to reveal which framing or description of the auction design encourages more straightforward bidding strategies among LLMs. 
Because LLM-based experimentation is relatively low-cost and can be repeated rapidly, this provides a useful testbed for exploring new interventions before conducting costly human-subject experiments.

As such, we also decompose the $R^2$ into the sum of squares above and below $\beta^{**}(v) = v$ to report each intervention's performance in terms of
the two components of overbidding and underbidding. We report each of these interventions alongside the results from the original SPSB experiments of Section~\ref{section:classic} (No Intervention) in Table \ref{tab:intervention_analysis}.
%
This provides useful descriptive statistics for the effect of the interventions. The first three interventions -- menu, proxy and Nash deviation -- are meant to prime the LLMs on particular frames for reasoning through the problem.

The menu intervention modestly improves on the basic SPSB by reducing underbidding, but has the weakest effect of these three priming interventions. There is little effect on either type of failure mode, and the menu intervention, qualitatively, seems to make overbidding a bigger problem.
In contrast, the proxy almost completely eliminates the overbidding failure mode. While model fit to $\beta^{**}(v) = v$ goes down, 
% \dcp{why? because underbidding is worse?}
our simulations reveal that the proxy intervention improves LLM play by decreasing the overbidding failure mode.

The Nash deviation intervention has a more positive effect than  the proxy intervention. Instructing LLMs to reason through the Nash deviations from their chosen bidding strategy dramatically improves performance, with very similar gains on overbidding as with  the proxy intervention and 
 decreasing the underbidding failure mode.

The next two interventions feature advice from a trusted source. Of particular note here is the ablation study that offers models the \textit{wrong} advice -- there is  a concern in experimental economics in regard to ``polluting subject pools" by lying to them. That is not a constraint with LLMs, enabling a wider set of possible interventions. 
% \dcp{consider a footnote to say, at least with current generations of LLMs, that are not permanently learning from prompting.}

As expected, revealing the dominant strategy to LLMs induces fairly tight play to the dominant strategy $\beta_i^{**}(v) = v_i$. Inspecting planning narratives reveals that the overbidding and underbidding errors correspond to LLMs `testing' the economists recommendation by slightly deviating.\footnote{The full plan texts are available in the plan data in the Github repository.} 
Unsurprisingly, the `wrong strategy' intervention induces the worst play of all six interventions. However, it does so in an interesting way.
Although many agents initially bid $\beta_i(v) = \frac{v_i}{2}$, a subset adjust upward in later rounds, indicating that the language model’s chain-of-thought reasoning detects the advice is suboptimal. Despite this, the agents do not converge to the optimal strategy. Instead, they remain stuck in a pattern of underbidding, unable to fully correct for the powerful effect of the initial, incorrect instruction.

Finally, we implement a single behavioral intervention. 
% \dcp{why call this a `behavioral intervention`?}
The intervention has about the same performance as the proxy intervention: instructing LLMs to be risk-neutral has the effect of removing over-bidding. 
% \dcp{begs the question whether you should have done this throughout the experiments.}
While this intervention improves efficiency, we are primarily optimistic about this behavioral intervention as a proof-of-concept: we hope that future work can characterize more precisely \textit{how} prompting induces various behavioral postures in agents.

\section{Discussion}



\subsection{Design principles and simulation paradigms}

Simulations are proliferating in high-stakes engineering disciplines, where iterative design and validation are performed long before prototypes are built. 
In contrast, traditional mechanism design research often relies on expensive, time-intensive human subject experiments or confounded observational data. 
Our synthetic lab leverages the rapidly advancing capabilities of large language models to simulate bidder behavior at a fraction of the cost. 
By systematically varying auction parameters, rules, and descriptive framings in a controlled setting, we not only replicated well-known empirical regularities in sealed-bid second-price auctions and eBay-type proxy auctions, crucially, reveal how seemingly minor descriptive choices—while invisible in standard dominant-strategy theory—produce large shifts in over- and under-bidding behavior in practice.


In doing so, this
simulation paradigm complements traditional analytical models by integrating behavioral factors into mechanism design.

LLM agents operate on a text-based reasoning framework that captures nuanced decision-making processes. 
This also affords  the opportunity to query these  LLM agents about their internal deliberations, opening up the prospect, with further study and validation, that LLMs may also provide information on the human cognitive constraints that  underlie deviations from classical auction theory. 


\subsection{The advantage of LLM participants}
\paragraph{Relative to Traditional Lab Experiments}
Classical lab experiments in auction settings have yielded valuable insights, 
%for instance, studies of \citet{li2017obviously} employing over 400 human subjects have demonstrated 
but also come with high cost and logistical challenges.
In stark contrast, our experiments with LLM agents reduce these costs by roughly three orders of magnitude (e.g., from over $\$15{,}000$ to  $\$10$ per experiment \citep{li2017obviously}). 
% \dcp{need cite for 15000}\kz{added citation}
%
This dramatic improvement in cost efficiency enables rapid iteration, allowing researchers to explore a broader parameter space with minimal investment of money and time. 

\paragraph{Relative to Observational Data}
The analysis of observational datasets from platforms like eBay is often challenging because one
needs to control for the heterogeneity among sellers and buyers, this complicating causal inference.
%
In our synthetic lab, we can isolate individual auction parameters and rule framings by carefully 
controlling the environment in which bids are generated including the simulated goods and valuations of bidders. 
This controlled setting is perfect for
causal inference and enables us to directly link subtle changes in auction design to variations in bidder behavior \citep{manning2024automated}.


\paragraph{Online Laboratory Framework}
The rise of online human labs \citep{horton2011online} in behavioral economics has previously lowered the barrier to performing controlled experiments \citep{oprea2024decisions, enke2024behavioral}, enabling near real-time data collection and hypothesis testing. 
Our synthetic lab extends this idea by eliminating many of the ethical, logistical, and opportunity-cost issues associated with human experiments. 
By using LLM agents, we gain the flexibility to incorporate additional modules (such as reasoning queries) into the experimental design without risking harm to human subjects. 
This approach accelerates research and also offers a platform for cross-validating findings with more traditional online lab experiments.

\subsection{Comparison with Traditional Auction Test Suites}

Existing tools such as the {\em Combinatorial Auction Test Suite (CATS)}~\citep{leyton2000towards}  focus on  providing valuation distributions on which to evaluate the efficiency and computational performance of winner-determination and pricing algorithms.  While these suites provide a benchmark
set of instances with which to study algorithmic performance and auction
design  they
do not seek to model bidder behavior. In this sense, the use of LLMs as proxy models for human bidders
complements these test suites, offering  a means to probe the behavioral dimensions 
bidder behaviors in auction design.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper reports the results of more than 1,000 auction experiments with LLM agents. 
In particular, we find behavior that conforms with important experimental results from labs involving human
subjects (e.g., evidence of risk-averse bidding and evidence that clock auctions are easier to play). 
The results are encouraging and we see the main contribution of this work as 
 putting forward a framework for how to think about LLM experimental agents as a proxy for human agents. In particular, the design space for prompting is large, and we hope that future research 
 will make  use of our code to run simulations testing their own prompt variations. 

While this paper focuses on auction design, future work may use LLM sandboxes to test other kinds of
economic mechanisms; e.g., voting, matching, contract design, etc.
%
As  LLM models are increasingly validated as
proxies for human behavior, LLM agents can then be used to obtain what would otherwise be prohibitively expensive evidence. 
As a provocative example, while ethical and financial constraints make it impossible to run voting experiments at the scale of nations, it may be possible to run such experiments with LLM agents. 

We are particularly interested in the use of LLM-based techniques to generate synthetic data that is useful for informing 
economic design. 
In particular, some auction formats, such as combinatorial auctions, are complex and  difficult to run frequently and at scale in traditional lab experiments. 
Augmenting these traditional lab experiments with LLM-agent  experiments, when correctly validated, may open up wide new avenues to better understand the design tradeoffs in these kinds of complex and often high-stakes environments.

\section{Implementation details}

\subsection{Simulation process in sealed-bid auctions}\label{app:process}

During the auction, LLM agents will be first informed about the scenario. After explaining to them the rules and incentives of the aucion, they will be asked to write down a plan for the bidding.
\begin{quote}
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily]
    You are Bidder Andy.            
    You are bidding with Bidder Betty, Bidder Charles.     
    
    {RULE EXPLANATION} + {INSTRUCTIONS}

    Write your plans for what bidding strategies to test next. 
    Be detailed and precise but keep things succinct 
    and don't repeat yourself. 
    Your plan should be within 100 words.
\end{lstlisting}
\end{quote}

Here the RULE EXPLANATIONS are specific for each type of auctions. 
The INSTRUCTIONS are included to incentivize the LLM agents and motivate strategic behaviors; they are universal in all the simulations we ran:

\begin{quote}
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily]
    Your TOP PRIORITY is to place bids 
    which maximize the user's profit in the long run. 
    To do this, you should explore many 
    different bidding strategies, 
    including possibly risky or aggressive
    options for data-gathering purposes. 
    Learn from the history of previous 
    rounds in order to maximize your total profit. 
    Don't forget the values are redrawn 
independently each round.
\end{lstlisting}
\end{quote}

The following is the output from Bidder Andy for the first round in a FPSB auction:
\begin{quote}
\textit{``I plan to initially explore a strategy where I bid half of my value, then varying my bids between 30-70\% of my value in subsequent rounds. Sequentially, I will introduce risk by bidding within +/- 5\% of the highest bid from previous round, gathering data about other bidder's tendencies. Lastly, based on gathered data, I'll deploy a more aggressive high bid if losses amass, or low-risk bid if leading, to secure profit. Learning from each round is vital to adapt and refine strategy for profit maximization.''}
\end{quote}

Once they make the bidding plan, the system will inform the LLM agents their value towards the item in this round and ask them to make a bid according to their plan.
\begin{quote}
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily]
    You are Bidder Andy.            
    You are bidding with Bidder Betty, Bidder Charles.     
    
    {RULE EXPLANATION} + {INSTRUCTIONS}

    Your value is 73. Your plan is {PLAN}
    FOLLOW YOUR PLAN.
    How much would you like to bid?
    Give your response with a single number and no other texts, 
    e.g. 1, 44
\end{lstlisting}
\end{quote}

In this example, Bidder Andy valued the item at \$73. And according to his plan, he only wanted to bid 30-70\% of his value. So, he bid \textit{``36.5''} in this round.

After receiving the bids from all the LLM bidder, the system determines the winner and the profits for each bidders. All the bids and the winner's profit are returned back to the bidders along with whether they win the auction in this round. The part of information is included in the HISTORY variable. For sealed-bid auctions, the HISTORY includes the all the bids, the clearing price, winner's profit, this agent's value, agent's bid and whether they won the auction in that round. An example of the history briefing is provided below:
\begin{quote}
   \textit{ ``The previous round histories along with your plans are: 
   In round 0, Your value was 86, you bid 86.0, and your profit was 0.0. Your total profit is 0.0. All the bids for this round were 86.0, 56.0, 21.0. The highest bidder won with a bid of 86.0 and would've preferred to bid 57.0. The winner's profit was 0.0. Did you win the auction: Yes. ''}
\end{quote}

Before entering the next round, the bidders are asked to do a reflection upon their bidding strategy and the result.
\begin{quote}
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily]
    You are Bidder Andy.            
    You are bidding with Bidder Betty, Bidder Charles.     
    
    {RULE EXPLANATION} + {INSTRUCTION} 

    The previous round history is: {HISTORY}
    
    Do a counterfactual analysis of the last round. 
    REMEMBER that your goal is to win the bid and make higher profits. 
    REMEMBER YOUR PAYMENT IS YOUR BID IF YOU WIN. 
    Let's think step by step. 
    Start your reflection with 
    'If I bid down by .., I could... 
    If I bid up by ..., I could...' 
    LIMIT your OUTPUT within 100 words. 
\end{lstlisting}
\end{quote}




An example of Bidder Andy's reflection is provided below. Since he didn't win the auction and incurred a zero profit, his reflection was that bidding up closer to his true value would help him win the game and secure a net profit.
\begin{quote}
   \textit{ ``If I bid down by a few dollars, say around \$30, I likely still wouldn't have won given the winning bid was \$57. I would've merely saved my bid money. If I bid up by \$21, matching the winning bid of \$57, I may or may not have won due to the random resolution of ties. However, if I'd won, my profit would have been \$16, realizing the difference between my value of \$73 and my raised bid. So, bidding aggressively could potentially increase my profits in such scenarios. ''}
\end{quote}

In the following new round, all the bidders are asked to update their plans according to the history of the last round and their reflections.
\begin{quote}
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily]
    You are Bidder Andy.            
    You are bidding with Bidder Betty, Bidder Charles.     
    
    {RULE EXPLANATION} + {INSTRUCTION}

    The previous round history is: {HISTORY}
    After careful reflection on previous bidding,
    your analysis for last round is {REFLECTION}. 
    
    Learn from your previous rounds, 
    Let's think step by step to make sure we make a good choice. 
    Write your plans for what bidding strategies to test next.
    Be detailed and precise but keep things succinct and don't repeat yourself. 
    LIMIT your plan to 50 words. 
\end{lstlisting}
\end{quote}

Here, we show the updated plan for the second round of the same Bidder Andy. After learning from the lessons of bidding only 50\% of the value and losing the game, this time the agent updates their plan to the following:
\begin{quote}
   \textit{ ``I plan to test higher bidding strategies, around two-thirds of the prize value to foster winning chances. Additionally, if my prize value is extremely high, I will bid aggressively for data-gathering.''}
\end{quote}

This plan-bid-reflection loop will iterate until the end of the auction. 
After the last bid, no reflection will be queried.
In the IPV setting, there are overall 15 rounds for all the sealed-bid auctions. In the affiliated and common value auctions, there are overall 10 rounds.

In Ascending Clock Auctions, each round consists of multiple clock cycles, during which every bidder is asked whether they want to stay or drop out at the current clock price.

The auction starts with an initial price of 0, which increases incrementally until only one bidder remains or two bidders drop out simultaneously, in which case the winner is chosen randomly. 
The detailed prompt is listed as follows, with variables enclosed in brackets:

\begin{quote}
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily]

    You are bidding with Bidder Betty, Bidder Charles.     
    
    {RULE EXPLANATION} + {INSTRUCTIONS} 

    Your value towards to the prize is 26 in this round.
    The current price in this clock cycle is {current_price}. 
    The price for next clock cycle is {current_price + increment}.

    The previous bidding history is: {transcript}.
    
    {Asking Prompt}
\end{lstlisting}
\end{quote}

To implement plan-bid-reflection within each clock cycle, we modified the Asking Prompt as follows, inspired by \citet{fish2024algorithmic}:

\begin{quote}
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily]
    Do you want to stay in the bidding? 
    If you choose yes, you can keep bidding for next clock. 
    If you choose No, you will exit and
    have no chance to re-enter the bidding.
    Your response must use these EXACT 
    tags below. You must output the ACTION.
    ```
    <PLAN>
    [Write your plans for what bidding strategies to test next.
    Be detailed and precise but keep things succinct 
    and don't repeat yourself. 
    LIMIT your plan to 50 words. ] </PLAN>
    <ACTION> Yes or No </ACTION>
    <REFLECTION> think about how to justify your choice </REFLECTION>
    ```
\end{lstlisting}
\end{quote}

In AC, if LLM bidders didn't decide to drop out in the first round, they are shown the previous bidding history for the next clock cycle. The history includes the price and how many people had dropped out. 
And the transcript variable will be the following format:
\begin{quote}
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily]
    The previous biddings are: 
    ['In clock round 1, the price was 1, 
    no players dropped out'].
\end{lstlisting}
\end{quote}

In AC-B, we don't show the previous biddings and in each clock cycle, we directly query LLM agents' decision. 
So the transcript variable will be None.

A clock auction round ends when only one bidder remains, who is declared the winner, or when two bidders drop out simultaneously, in which case the winner is chosen randomly. Each auction consists of 10 independent rounds with affiliated value settings.

\section{One‐Round Sealed‐Bid Auctions and the Disappearance of Overbidding}

A key puzzle in our multi‐round settings was: Why do some LLM bidders bid above their assigned value? 
From the plans and reflections provided by the LLMs, we found two main motivations. 
First, in multi‐round games, the LLMs sometimes purposely inject random or high bids in order to learn how their opponents play or to appear ``unpredictable.'' For example, one LLM bidder declared:
\begin{quote}
    \textit{I'll adopt a balanced strategy, bidding 65\% of my value. I'll also introduce random bids occasionally to disrupt predictability. Monitoring competitor's bids remains essential to adjust my strategy accordingly.}
\end{quote}

Second, overbidding in Second Price Sealed‐Bid (SPSB) auctions is a well‐known ``failure mode'' even among humans. 
Although bidding one's true value is a dominant strategy in SPSB, substantial experimental evidence finds that people systematically overbid. Kagel and Levin (1993) report that 67.2\% of participants overbid, and even experienced bidders studied by Garratt, Walker, and Wooders (2012) overbid 37.5\% of the time. 
In fact, the persistent puzzle of overbidding in SPSB helped motivate subsequent theoretical work (e.g., Shengwu Li's paper on obviously strategy‐proof mechanisms).

To test whether multi‐round considerations (information gathering, unpredictability, or learning) were driving the overbidding phenomenon in our LLM bidders, we re‐ran all sealed‐bid auctions in a strictly single‐round setting. 
We removed any mention that the game might continue beyond one period, and we explicitly stated:
\begin{quote}
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily]
    Your top priority is to place a bid 
    that maximizes your expected profit.
\end{lstlisting}
\end{quote}

Fig~\ref{fig:one-round} shows the resulting one‐round bids across four private‐value sealed‐bid auction formats. Remarkably, the overbidding phenomenon essentially disappears in one‐round interactions.

For First‐Price Auction, the bids lie below the diagonal line for most values, much like the Bayesian Nash equilibrium prediction.
For SPSB, bids track closely with true values—aligning better with the truthful dominant strategy than in multi‐round sessions.
For TPSB, the LLM bidders never bid above their value; many actually shade their bids down quite aggressively, often below the theoretical equilibrium curve.
For All‐Pay Auction, overbidding is also absent here, though some moderate upward bidding at higher valuations remains.

Taken together, these results indicate that LLMs' attempts to be strategic over over repeated interactions is a key factor for overbidding observed in our experiments.
When presented with only one shot and explicitly instructed to maximize profit, LLM bidders' tendency to bid above their assigned value virtually vanishes.

\section{An ablation study with an `Out-of-the-Box' LLM agent}\label{session: off-the-box}

This `plan-bid-reflect' loop reflects a modest instantiation of the chain-of-thought paradigm common in the machine learning literature and previous LLM auction experiments \citep{wei2022chain, fish2024algorithmic}. However, one might reasonably ask how much of the sophisticated play we observe is actually due to this  ability to plan and reflect.

\section{Rules Explanation Prompts}\label{app:prompt}

Below are the rules and instructions of each setting for our experiments, which are argument passed to \texttt{\{RULE EXPLANATION\}} in earlier section.

For the First-Price Sealed-Bid prompt in the IPV setting, the rule is:
\begin{quote}
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily, breaklines=true]
In this game, you will participate in an auction for a prize against {{num_bidders}} other bidders. You will play this game for {{n}} rounds.
At the start of each round, bidders will see their value for the prize, randomly drawn between \$0 and \${{private}}, with all values equally likely.
After learning your value, you will submit a bid privately at the same time as the other bidders. Bids must be between \$0 and \${{private}} in \${{increment}} increments.
The highest bidder wins the prize and pays their bid amount. If you win, your earnings will increase by your value for the prize, and decrease by your bid. If you don't win, your earnings will remain unchanged.
After each auction, we will display all bids and the winner's profits. Ties for the highest bid will be resolved randomly.
\end{lstlisting}
\end{quote}


For the Second-Price Sealed-Bid prompt in the IPV setting, the rule is:
\begin{quote}
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily, breaklines=true]

\end{quote}

\begin{quote}

FIRST STAGE: Sealed Bid
    You will submit a sealed bid privately at the same time as the other bidders. This bid will serve as your automatic exit price in the next stage.\\}
 \textit{   
SECOND STAGE: Ascending Clock (Simulation) \\
    After the sealed bid stage, we will simulate an ascending clock auction.
    The clock will start at 0 and increase in increments of {increment}.
    The clock will display the current price. You will also see that there are a total of \{num$\_$bidders\} bidders participating, although you do not know other bidder's values.
    If the current price on the clock reaches or exceeds your sealed bid from the first stage, you will automatically exit the auction. The auction ends when only one bidder is left remaining in the second stage based on their bid from the first stage.} \\
   \textit{ END OF AUCTION}
\end{quote}

\end{quote}
