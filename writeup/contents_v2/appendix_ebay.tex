\section{eBay Environment: Details and Hidden Reserves}\label{app:ebay}

The eBay-style environment provides the paper's field-validation exhibit: LLM bidders snipe under a hard close and stop sniping under a soft close, in the same qualitative direction as the human field evidence (Section~\ref{sec:validation}, Figure~\ref{fig:ebay-sniping}). This appendix collects the environment details, treatment-level timing detail, and the hidden-reserve revenue analysis, which we relocate from the main text because its result is an across-the-board null in a calibration where reserves rarely bind---and where, as the audit below documents, the simulator's proxy-price rule disables the main mechanical channel through which a reserve would raise revenue.

\subsection{Environment}\label{app:ebay-env}

The stylized eBay environment (full simulation loop in Appendix~\ref{app:ebay-process}) features $n=3$ bidders, proxy bidding, and $T_{\text{eBay}}=10$ discrete bidding periods (``days''), with independent private values $v_i \sim \mathrm{Unif}[0,99]$. Each bidder maintains a \emph{maximum bid}; the platform automatically raises the standing bid only as needed to keep the current leader ahead, and each day every bidder may increase their maximum bid or hold. We vary two design dimensions---hidden reserve price and closing rule---yielding four treatments: (T1) standard proxy bidding with a hard close; (T2) proxy bidding with a soft-close (auction-extension) rule; (T3) proxy bidding with a hidden reserve; (T4) both. A hidden reserve is a seller-chosen minimum acceptable sale price that is \emph{not disclosed} to bidders; if the highest maximum bid fails to meet it, the item does not sell. For hidden-reserve treatments we consider $r \in \{40, 50, 60\}$. Each treatment cell comprises $30$ independent runs (GPT-4o, $T=0.5$; $29$ runs for the soft-close $r=60$ cell, one run missing from the archived logs). The T3/T4 rule prompts differ from T1/T2 only by the reserve paragraph:

\subsection{Timing detail across treatments}\label{app:ebay-timing}

Figure~\ref{fig:ebay-cdf} shows the empirical distribution functions of final-winning-bid time for all four treatments, complementing the pooled histograms of Figure~\ref{fig:ebay-sniping}. Both soft-close treatments shift decisively earlier than both hard-close treatments, and the hard-close CDFs display the characteristic deadline mass. One claim from the source draft does \emph{not} survive the recomputation from the recovered logs: adding a hidden reserve on top of the soft close (T4) does not dampen last-period activity beyond the soft close alone (T2). Under the paper's timing definition the ordering is, if anything, reversed (share of decisive bids on or after the scheduled final day: $21.3\%$ in T4 versus $10.0\%$ in T2; Mann--Whitney $p = 0.048$), and under an alternative price-based timing proxy the two treatments are indistinguishable ($7.9\%$ versus $6.7\%$). We therefore claim only that both soft-close treatments strongly attenuate sniping relative to the hard close, with no further attenuation from the reserve.

\subsection{Hidden reserve prices have limited revenue impact in this calibration}\label{app:ebay-reserve}

The use of reserve prices in auctions is well understood in auction theory: \citet{myerson1981optimal} showed that an optimally chosen reserve maximizes expected revenue in regular private-value settings. eBay additionally allows sellers (for a fee) to set a \emph{hidden} reserve---a threshold bidders do not observe until it is met by the highest maximum bid. While some work suggests a hidden reserve can raise expected revenue relative to no reserve in common-value settings \citep{vincent1995bidding}, in a private-values environment with rational bidders using proxy bidding, hiding the reserve should not change behavior relative to a known reserve or no reserve: bidders should still submit their maximum value upfront. A large empirical study of hidden reserves on eBay likewise finds that bidders change their behavior little in the face of hidden versus non-hidden reserves \citep{einav2011learning}.

Our simulations echo this limited sensitivity in behavior and outcome. Focusing on seller revenue, Figure~\ref{fig:ebay-revenue} shows revenue across all eight auction types, and Table~\ref{tab:ebay-revenue} reports all pairwise two-sided $t$-tests, recomputed from the recovered run logs. Across the baseline proxy auction (T1), the soft-close variant (T2), the hidden-reserve variants (T3), and the combined design (T4), mean revenue ranges only from $57.3$ to $58.0$, and we do not detect statistically meaningful revenue differences in our calibration: every $|t|$ is below $0.15$, and nonparametric checks agree (pairwise Mann--Whitney minimum $p = 0.82$; Kruskal--Wallis $H = 0.11$, $p \approx 1.0$).

\paragraph{Honest framing: the reserves straddle the Myerson level but rarely bind---and the simulator disables the price-lift channel.} For $v \sim \mathrm{Unif}[0,99]$ and a seller who values the item at zero, the Myerson-optimal reserve solves $r^{\star} = \bigl(1-F(r^{\star})\bigr)/f(r^{\star})$, giving $r^{\star} = 49.5$ \citep{myerson1981optimal}. Our grid $r \in \{40, 50, 60\}$ therefore straddles the optimal level---the reserves are not simply ``too low.'' They are, however, rarely \emph{pivotal} in this calibration. Under truthful proxy bidding with three bidders, the reserve blocks the sale (all maximum bids below $r$) with probability $(r/99)^{3}$, i.e., approximately $6.6\%$, $12.9\%$, and $22.2\%$ at $r = 40, 50, 60$; the recovered logs come in at or below these predictions, with the reserve blocking the sale in $0/30$, $2/30$, and $4/30$ hard-close runs at $r = 40, 50, 60$ ($0/30$, $2/30$, and $3/29$ under the soft close).

A reserve can also matter without blocking the sale, by lifting the closing price whenever the second-highest maximum bid falls below $r$ while the highest meets it. An audit of the recovered logs shows that this channel is \emph{mechanically disabled} in our simulator: in the runs where the price was liftable in this sense ($6$, $8$, and $13$ of $30$ hard-close runs at $r = 40, 50, 60$, with the same pattern under the soft close), the final price reached the reserve in $0$, $0$, and $2$ cases---and the two exceptions are increment artifacts (a second-highest maximum of $59$ mapping to a closing price of $60$). Throughout, the closing price is pinned at the second-highest maximum bid plus one increment, whereas eBay proper lifts the standing price to a met reserve. The revenue null in Figure~\ref{fig:ebay-revenue} and Table~\ref{tab:ebay-revenue} therefore partly reflects an implementation channel being switched off, not only bidder insensitivity to hidden reserves; this caveat should accompany any use of the null. Under the economically strict revenue convention (unsold items yield zero revenue), mean revenue falls to $54.9$ at $r=50$ and $51.7$ at $r=60$ under the hard close, but no comparison against the baseline approaches significance (Welch $p \ge 0.37$).

\paragraph{Interpretation.} The hidden-reserve null is consistent with the field evidence of \citet{einav2011learning} and with rational-bidder theory under private values, but in this calibration it is a weak test, for the binding-frequency and price-lift reasons above. We therefore treat the eBay environment's evidentiary weight as resting on the timing results of Section~\ref{sec:validation}---where the design lever (the closing rule) demonstrably binds and LLM behavior moves in the humans' direction---and report the reserve analysis here for completeness.
