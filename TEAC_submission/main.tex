\begin{abstract}
This paper investigates the behavior of large language models (LLMs) in auctions, where multiple bidders interact under explicit rules and incentives. We ask which features of human behavior general-purpose models preserve when used out of the box. We evaluate five LLMs across seven laboratory settings against human benchmarks reconstructed from published experiments, with uncertainty bands for the private-value comparisons. Our main focus is on three large models without extended test-time reasoning\rev{, which we compare with a reasoning model and a small open-weight model}. LLM and human deviations from theory differ in magnitude and often in direction: humans overbid in second-price auctions, whereas most models that deviate underbid. Without task-specific fine-tuning or calibration to human bids, the three non-reasoning large models preserve the main orderings of auction formats by deviation from theory. First-price auctions are harder than second-price, and ascending clocks reduce deviations relative to sealed bids\rev{ for every model whose sealed bids deviate from the benchmark}. Kendall's $\tau_b$ between the human difficulty ranking and that of \rev{our reference non-reasoning model} is \rev{$\TauFiveGpt$ and positive in \TauFivePGpt\% of joint bootstrap draws}. The reasoning model bids almost at equilibrium in the observed private-value settings, leaving little variation in errors to compare; the small model's large errors yield an inverted ranking. All five models nevertheless reproduce the stronger first-price winner's curse. Clock framing improves bidding for two of the three non-reasoning large models, and \rev{the reference model} recovers the ordering of last-minute bidding across closing rules in an eBay-style marketplace.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:introduction}

Rapidly advancing capabilities of large language models (LLMs) raise an exciting possibility: might LLMs serve as proxies for human bidders in the development of economic mechanisms, thereby making experiments orders of magnitude cheaper to run \citep{aher2023using, horton2023large, manning2024automated}?
The scientific question we address here is whether, and when, LLMs provide good human proxies in auctions.
If they do, this would be practically useful: LLM simulations cost orders of magnitude less than experiments with human subjects, scale up easily, and are much faster to run.
A mechanism designer choosing between two auction formats needs to know how bidders will actually play them, not only what theory prescribes.
Decades of laboratory experiments show that the two can differ systematically: people bid above value in second-price auctions, shade too little in first-price auctions, fall prey to the winner's curse when values are common, and play the same strategy-proof rule far more truthfully when it is implemented as an ascending clock than as a sealed bid \citep{kagel1993independent, kagel1986winner, li2017obviously, breitmoser2022obviousness}.
We distinguish three features of behavior that a simulation could preserve: the \emph{magnitude} of the deviation from theory, its \emph{direction} (under- or over-bidding), and the \emph{ordering} of mechanisms by how far bidders deviate, together with the signs of the comparative statics between them.
The three tiers are progressively less demanding, and the third is the one a designer comparing candidate mechanisms actually uses.

Auctions are a demanding benchmark for simulated bidders because the human evidence is unusually sharp.
Theory gives exact predictions: a Bayes--Nash bid function in first-price auctions and dominant-strategy truth-telling in second-price and clock formats \citep{myerson1981optimal, krishna2009auction}.
The experimental literature documents not only that people deviate from these predictions but by how much more they deviate in some mechanisms than in others \citep{kagel2020handbook}: bidding above the risk-neutral equilibrium in first-price auctions \citep{coppinger1980incentives, cox1988theory}, bidding above value in second-price auctions \citep{kagel1993independent}, winner's-curse losses in common-value auctions \citep{bazerman1983won, kagel1986winner}, more truthful play in ascending-clock than in sealed-bid implementations of the same rule \citep{li2017obviously, breitmoser2022obviousness}, and last-minute bidding under hard-close rules in online marketplaces \citep{roth2002last, ockenfels2006late}.
This body of evidence supplies an ordering of behavioral difficulty across mechanisms, which is exactly the object a designer needs and exactly what a simulation can be tested against.
A growing literature evaluates LLMs in strategic settings against theoretical optimality; we instead evaluate them against these human regularities, with theory as the reference point rather than the criterion.

The benchmark requires human results that are comparable across mechanisms, and the literature does not report them in that form.
Published studies report moments rather than micro data, such as overbidding shares, bid-on-value regressions, and mean absolute deviations, from experiments that differ in the number of bidders, value supports, and repetition.
In comparing with the behavior of human participants, we adopt the method of moments to estimate human bidder strategies in each of several studies from experimental economics.
With these estimated strategies, we populate a data-generating process that enables direct comparison of LLM bids to those inferred from decades of human laboratory experiments.
Using a unified metric---the {\em scaled mean absolute deviation (SMAD)} from the theoretical benchmark---we study LLM behavior across first-price and second-price, sealed-bid and dynamic auction formats, and for different value environments.
Because the fitted parameters are uncertain, we carry that uncertainty forward as reconstruction bands and use the human anchors only for orderings, directions, and comparative statics.
On the simulation side we hold the prompt fixed: every bidder receives an induced value and a single objective, to maximize expected profit, following the induced-value tradition of experimental economics \citep{smith1976experimental}.
What varies is the mechanism (first-, second-, and third-price sealed bids, open and closed ascending clocks), the information structure (independent, affiliated, and common values), and the model.
Our main focus is on three large models without extended test-time reasoning: GPT-4o, Claude~3.5 Haiku, and Gemini~2.0 Flash. We include GPT-5-mini, a reasoning model that allocates extended test-time computation to each decision, to ask whether more accurate equilibrium play also makes a model a better simulator of human behavior. The small open-weights model Llama-3-8B-Instruct provides a comparison with more limited capabilities.
We use all five models out of the box, without task-specific fine-tuning or calibration to human bids. This complements work on models specifically trained to predict human behavior, such as Centaur, which is fine-tuned on a large collection of human experimental choices \citep{binz2025foundation}.
For the deeper distributional diagnostics we adopt GPT-4o as a single reference model, to make comparisons across mechanisms and environments interpretable and stable, and because GPT-4o remains a widely used, well-studied general-purpose model for strategic and economic reasoning tasks in the recent literature \citep{horton2023large, manning2024automated}.
Our goal is not to claim that GPT-4o is the best available model at the time of writing, but rather to use a strong, widely deployed baseline to probe a methodological question.

The results differ by tier.
Magnitudes do not match: no model's SMAD falls within the human reconstruction band in more than two of the five private-value formats; GPT-4o deviates more than humans in five of seven formats and far less in the clock formats, GPT-5-mini plays the equilibrium almost exactly, and Llama-3-8B deviates several times more than humans everywhere.
Directions do not match: human bidders in second-price auctions predominantly bid above value \citep{kagel1993independent, li2017obviously}, whereas GPT-4o, Gemini, and Llama predominantly bid below value, GPT-5-mini bids at value, and only Claude~3.5 Haiku errs in the human direction.
Although they receive no task-specific training to mimic human bidders, the non-reasoning large models preserve the main orderings: first-price auctions are harder than second-price for GPT-4o, Claude, and Gemini as they are for humans, the clock implementation reduces deviations \rev{for every model whose sealed bids leave room to do so}, and the first-price winner's curse exceeds the second-price curse for all five models.
Kendall's $\tau_b$ between the human and GPT-4o difficulty rankings over five formats is \rev{$\TauFiveGpt$ and positive in \TauFivePGpt\% of joint bootstrap draws}, and the ordering is robust to the sampling temperature (Appendix~\ref{app:temperature}).
The ranking is uninformative for GPT-5-mini, whose cells all sit at the benchmark, and inverted for Llama.

These comparisons show why more accurate equilibrium play need not make a model a better simulator of human behavior.
GPT-5-mini bids almost at equilibrium in the observed private-value settings, leaving little variation in errors with which to compare human difficulty across formats\rev{; its clock exits are likewise within one price increment of value}.
Llama-3-8B makes large errors whose ordering is opposite to the human one.
The non-reasoning large models instead make systematic errors that vary across mechanisms in a similar order to human errors, even when their magnitudes and directions differ.
Thus, accuracy relative to theory and fidelity to human behavior are distinct criteria.

We then use the ordinal result the way a designer would.
First as a prediction: describing a sealed-bid second-price auction as a clock raises truthful play among human subjects \citep{breitmoser2022obviousness}, so it should raise truthful play for the non-reasoning large models.
It does for GPT-4o and Claude~3.5 Haiku, and it fails only for Gemini~2.0 Flash, whose baseline play in this format is already close to truthful, which is where the argument predicts no gain.
Second in a market structure documented by field data rather than by laboratory experiments: in an eBay-style proxy-bidding marketplace, LLM bidders place $63\%$ of decisive bids in the last scheduled period under a hard close and $7\%$ under a soft-close extension rule, reproducing the ordering of last-minute bidding across closing rules observed on eBay and early Amazon \citep{roth2002last, ockenfels2006late}.

The paper makes three contributions.
It provides a benchmark that converts published human auction moments into cross-mechanism comparable quantities with explicit reconstruction uncertainty, so that any simulated bidder can be scored against decades of experimental evidence.
It provides a three-tier evaluation that separates what a simulation can be read for from what it cannot, and applies it to five models across seven formats.
And it turns the result into a concrete recommendation: LLM panels can screen candidate mechanisms and presentations for cross-mechanism orderings and the signs of comparative statics, but they should not be read for the magnitude or direction of human deviations. By an \emph{LLM panel}, we mean a set of models evaluated separately on the same auction settings, so that agreement across models can be checked. Our results support using the non-reasoning large models for this purpose; the reasoning and small models show why the panel's ability to reproduce human orderings must be validated.
Our work sits within the literature that uses LLMs as simulated agents in economics and social science \citep{aher2023using, park2023generative, horton2023large, manning2024automated}, a literature that also documents the limits of LLMs as stand-ins for human respondents \rev{\citep{santurkar2023whose, wang2025large}}.
Within auctions it complements three strands: work that treats LLMs as market participants in their own right and studies their collusion, information use, and trainability \citep{fish2024algorithmic, chen2023put, infobid2025, lu2025claude, tsuchihashi2023homo, auctionnet2024}; work that brings LLM bid distributions closer to human ones by adjusting them on a small human sample \citep{corrigan2025usefulness}, which asks a question downstream of ours; and work that designs auctions for LLM-powered platforms \citep{duetting2024mechanism, ramage2024adauctions}.
Markets populated by AI agents themselves are an important adjacent agenda in which LLM behavior is designer-relevant in its own right; our question is when LLM-generated behavior can inform design for human participants.
We do not learn mechanisms, as automated mechanism design does \citep{dutting2024optimal, curry102automated, wang2024gemnet, feng2018deep, wang2025bundleflow, ravindranath2023data}; we provide a benchmark for the behavioral inputs such pipelines would need, and a diagnostic for which of those inputs an LLM panel can supply.
\rev{Our reading of the simulations at the level of orderings follows \citet{fish2025econevals}, who evaluate the tendencies of LLMs in economic decisions, as distinct from their capabilities, and view the resulting scores as supporting ordinal comparisons between models that generalize, rather than cardinal scores that carry across contexts.}
A broader literature connects LLMs with game-theoretic modeling of strategic environments \citep{daskalakis2024charting, huang2025accelerated, soumalias2025llm, jiang2025incentive, wang2026llm}.

We have developed a code repository to systematically run auction experiments with any number of bidders and arbitrary prompting, flexible enough to generate synthetic data for many different auction formats.
For the experiments described in this paper, we ran more than 1,000 auctions with more than 5,000 GPT-4o agent participants for costs totaling less than \$400, and ran the same auctions with the other four models.
We release our experimental framework as an open-source benchmark for evaluating economic reasoning in LLMs.\footnote{Code and data: \url{https://github.com/KeHang-Zhu/llm-auction}.}

\section{Methods}\label{sec:general_method}
\subsection{Human Experiment Data Collection}

The experimental auctions literature is large but fragmented: each study reports its own summary statistics (overbidding frequencies, bid-on-value regressions, mean absolute deviations from the dominant strategy) for its own environment (number of bidders, value support, rounds, interface). To compare LLM behavior with this evidence \emph{across} mechanisms, we treat the published moments as the data. For each source we fit a simple model of individual bidding to the reported moments, simulate bids from the fitted model, and evaluate the same statistic that we compute for LLM bids: the scaled mean absolute deviation from theory defined in Section~\ref{sec:method}. This puts results from different studies on one scale across mechanisms. The reconstruction is not a substitute for micro data, so we carry its uncertainty forward as reconstruction bands (Appendix~\ref{app:bands}) and use the reconstructed benchmarks only for orderings, directions, and comparative statics, never for level comparisons or hypothesis tests.

\paragraph{Reconstructing human bid distributions via moment matching.}
Because micro-level bid data are rarely available from published experiments, we reconstruct synthetic human bid distributions by fitting a mixture model to aggregate statistics
reported in each source. We model bids as:
\[
b = b^\star_m(v) \cdot r
\]
where $b^\star_m(v)$ is the equilibrium bid and $r$ is a bid-to-equilibrium ratio drawn from a three-component mixture:
\begin{align*}
\text{With probability } p_{\text{eq}}: \quad & r \sim \mathcal{N}(1, \sigma^2) \\
\text{With probability } p_{\text{over}}: \quad & r \sim 1 + \text{Exp}(\lambda) \\
\text{With probability } p_{\text{under}}: \quad & r \sim \text{Uniform}(1-\delta, 1)
\end{align*}
This structure maps directly to how experimental papers categorize behavior: equilibrium bidders ($r \approx 1$), overbidders ($r > 1$), and underbidders ($r < 1$).\footnote{Quantal Response Equilibrium predicts symmetric deviations around equilibrium, contradicting the well-documented asymmetric overbidding in second-price auctions \citep{kagel1993independent}. The mixture model directly maps to how experimental papers categorize and report behavior.}

\paragraph{Source papers and equilibrium benchmarks.}
We draw on four sources: \citet{li2017obviously} and \citet{breitmoser2022obviousness} for second-price and ascending clock auctions; \citet{kagel1993independent} for first-price,
second-price, and third-price sealed-bid auctions with $n \in \{5, 10\}$ bidders; and \citet{gonczarowski2023strategyproofness} for second-price auctions comparing mechanism
descriptions. For each format, we use the risk-neutral Bayes-Nash equilibrium:
\begin{align*}
b^\star_{\text{SPSB}}(v) &= v & &\text{(second-price, ascending clock)} \\
b^\star_{\text{FPSB}}(v) &= \tfrac{n-1}{n} v & &\text{(first-price)} \\
b^\star_{\text{TPSB}}(v) &= \tfrac{n-1}{n-2} v & &\text{(third-price)}
\end{align*}

\paragraph{Parameter identification.}
Mixture weights $(p_{\text{eq}}, p_{\text{over}}, p_{\text{under}})$ are set directly from reported rates. For example, \rev{in the histogram of \citet[Figure~2(a)]{li2017obviously} for the sealed second-price auction, $39\%$ of groups have a second-highest bid within \$2 of the second-highest value, $39\%$ above it, and $22\%$ below it,
yielding $p_{\text{eq}} = 0.39$, $p_{\text{over}} = 0.39$, $p_{\text{under}} = 0.22$.}

The overbid scale $\lambda$ is chosen to match a secondary moment:
\begin{itemize}
  \item For \citet{li2017obviously} and \citet{breitmoser2022obviousness}: \rev{none; the anchor is the reported mean absolute deviation itself, and the mixture weights enter only the reconstruction bands (Appendix~\ref{app:bands}).}
  \item For \citet{kagel1993independent}: the regression coefficient $\beta$ from bid regressions.
  \item For \citet{gonczarowski2023strategyproofness}: mean absolute deviation (MAD $\approx$ \$0.51--0.55).
\end{itemize}
The equilibrium noise $\sigma$ is derived from regression $R^2$ where reported, via $\sigma^2 = \beta^2 \text{Var}(v)(1-R^2)/R^2$. The underbid bound $\delta$ is set consistently across
papers; we fix it at a common value because papers do not report underbid tail shapes separately.

\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{Conflicting moments in \citet{kagel1993independent}.}\label{sec:kl93_conflict}
For the first- and second-price sealed-bid auctions, the statistics reported by \citet{kagel1993independent} conflict with one another, and the conflict cannot be removed by a simple adjustment. The bid-on-value regression and its $R^2$ determine the mean and the dispersion of bids at each value; the shares of bids below, at, and above value determine how bids are distributed around value. In the first-price auction with $N=5$, the regression line $\hat b=1.14+0.92x$ lies almost on the 45-degree line (mean bid-to-value ratio $1.00$), and $R^2=0.88$ implies a residual standard deviation of about \$2.8 on the article's value support. A linear model with symmetric noise that matches these two statistics puts about half of all bids above value, while the article reports $0.4\%$. In the second-price auction, $27.0\%$ of bids are reported to be exactly at value, a point mass that no continuous noise distribution produces, and the regression with its $R^2$ implies about $20\%$ of bids below value against the reported $5.7\%$. The two sets of statistics may come from different subsamples or reflect bidding whose dispersion varies with value, but the article does not report enough to model either. We therefore do not estimate the reconstruction from all reported moments jointly. We set its parameters instead: the mean bid-to-value ratio follows the regression, the mixture weights follow the reported shares, and the remaining shape parameters ($\sigma$, $\lambda$, $\delta$) are fixed by us (Appendix~\ref{app:mixture}). This choice gives the anchors $24.8\%$ for first price and $5.6\%$ for second price. The choice moves the levels but not the ordering: fitting only the regression and $R^2$ with Gaussian noise gives $29.7\%$ and $12.4\%$, and first price is again harder than second price. Because these two anchors rest on a parameter choice and not on an estimate, we use them only for orderings and directions. The reconstruction bands below vary the inputs within their reported precision; they do not cover the choice of which moments to match, whose size the alternative fit above indicates.
\end{revblock}

Full parameter values appear in Appendix~\ref{app:mixture}.

\paragraph{Reconstruction uncertainty.}
Beyond Monte Carlo noise at fixed mixture parameters, the fitted parameters themselves are uncertain: reported rates are rounded, regression coefficients carry standard errors, and the underbid bound $\delta$ is fixed by convention. Appendix~\ref{app:bands} reports a parametric bootstrap over the admissible ranges of every mixture parameter ($B=2000$). The resulting 95\% reconstruction bands are shown as whiskers on the human bars in Figure~\ref{fig:comparison} and in brackets in Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison}. The two human orderings our comparisons rely on survive the bands: first-price deviations exceed second-price deviations in 100\% of re-draws, and the ascending clock improves on sealed second-price bidding in 100\% (open clock) and \rev{96.5\%} (closed clock) of re-draws.

A benchmark observation is therefore a published statistic for one experimental treatment, not a participant. \subsection{\texorpdfstring{Unified Deviation Metric across Auctions}{Unified Deviation Metric across Auctions}}\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Unified deviation metric (applied to all bids).}
Consider an auction \emph{environment} and \emph{format} indexed by $m$.
The index $m$ collects all primitives needed to define the theoretical benchmark, including the number of bidders $N$, the value (or signal) distribution, the information structure (independent private value/common value), and the mechanism rules (e.g., first-price vs.\ second-price, sealed-bid vs.\ clock).
Let $\mathcal I_i$ denote bidder $i$'s information at the moment of bidding under environment $m$ (e.g., $\mathcal I_i=v_i$ in private values, or $\mathcal I_i=s_i$ in common values).
Let $b_{i}$ denote an \emph{observed submitted bid} by bidder $i$ (we apply the definition to \emph{all bids}, not only winning bids), and let $b_m^\star(\mathcal I_i)$ denote the \emph{theoretical benchmark bid} under $m$ given information $\mathcal I_i$ (Bayes--Nash equilibrium in non-strategy-proof formats; truthful/value bidding in strategy-proof formats).

\noindent We define the absolute bid error for a bid with information $\mathcal I_i$ as
\[
d_m(\mathcal I_i)\;\equiv\;\bigl|b_i - b_m^\star(\mathcal I_i)\bigr|.
\]
To compare deviations across formats on a common scale, we normalize by the expected benchmark bid level,
\[
\mu_m^\star \;\equiv\; \mathbb E_m\!\left[b_m^\star(\mathcal I)\right],
\]
where $\mathbb E_m[\cdot]$ denotes expectation with respect to the distribution of the information object $\mathcal I$ induced by environment $m$ (i.e., the value/signal distribution and any randomization in the experimental design).
We then define the \emph{scaled mean absolute deviation} (SMAD) for auction format $m$ as
\[
\Delta_m \;\equiv\; 100 \cdot \frac{\mathbb E_m\!\left[d_m(\mathcal I)\right]}{\mu_m^\star}
\;=\; 100 \cdot \frac{\mathbb E_m\!\left[\left|b-b_m^\star(\mathcal I)\right|\right]}{\mathbb E_m\!\left[b_m^\star(\mathcal I)\right]}.
\]
This metric is interpretable as a percentage of the expected benchmark bid: $\Delta_m=10$ means that, on average, bids deviate from the benchmark by an amount equal to $10\%$ of the expected benchmark bid.

\paragraph{What the human literature says about the ordering of SMAD.}
The value of a common scale is that the experimental literature's conclusions about \emph{relative} difficulty become testable statements about SMAD. Three such conclusions anchor our benchmark. First, implementation matters even when incentives do not: the same strategy-proof second-price rule is played more truthfully as an ascending clock than as a sealed bid \citep{li2017obviously, breitmoser2022obviousness}. In the reconstructed anchors, SMAD is $3.5\%$ in the open clock and \rev{$5.5\%$} in the closed clock against $9.3\%$ in the sealed-bid version of the same affiliated-values environment. Second, a dominant strategy is easier to play than an equilibrium that requires bid shading: second-price bidders deviate far less than first-price bidders in the same independent-private-values environment ($5.6\%$ against $24.8\%$, a $4.4\times$ gap; \citealp{kagel1993independent}). Third, common-value environments, in which a bidder must condition on the adverse-selection content of winning, are harder than private-value environments with the same payment rule ($47.6\%$ against $24.8\%$ under first-price rules and $18.2\%$ against $5.6\%$ under second-price rules), and within common values the first-price winner's curse is more severe than the second-price curse \citep{kagel1986winner, levin1996revenue}. Ordered by human SMAD, the seven formats therefore run from first-price common value ($47.6\%$) through first-price IPV ($24.8\%$), second-price common value ($18.2\%$), sealed second-price APV ($9.3\%$), \rev{second-price IPV ($5.6\%$), and the closed clock ($5.5\%$)} to the open clock ($3.5\%$). It is this ordering, and the signs of the three design contrasts behind it, that we test LLM bidders against; the magnitudes themselves carry the reconstruction uncertainty discussed above.


Given a sample of bids $\{(b_k,\mathcal I_k)\}_{k=1}^n$ from format $m$, the plug-in estimator is
\[
\widehat{\Delta}_m
\;=\;
100 \cdot
\frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|b_k-b_m^\star(\mathcal I_k)\right|}
{\widehat{\mu}_m^\star},
\qquad
\widehat{\mu}_m^\star \equiv \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n} b_m^\star(\mathcal I_k).
\]
When we report a standard error for the (unscaled) mean absolute deviation $\frac{1}{n}\sum_k |b_k-b_m^\star(\mathcal I_k)|$, we propagate it to $\widehat{\Delta}_m$ by scaling with $\widehat{\mu}_m^\star$. We construct $95\%$ confidence intervals using a normal approximation when an analytic standard error is available.


\paragraph{Deterministic benchmarks.}
SMAD requires a single-valued benchmark mapping $\mathcal I \mapsto b_m^\star(\mathcal I)$.
Accordingly, we restrict attention to auction formats where the theoretical prediction is deterministic given the bidder’s information.

\subsection{LLM Data Generation}
\label{sec:data_generation}

We generate synthetic auction data by simulating auctions in which $n$ large language model (LLM) agents bid against one another under fully specified information structures and auction rules. Each experimental \emph{setting} is defined by (i) a \emph{value model} (how bidder values/signals are drawn), (ii) a \emph{mechanism environment} (the action space, allocation and payment rules, and the information revealed during play), and (iii) a \emph{prompt framing} (how the mechanism is described to the bidder). For each setting, we repeat the simulation $K$ times with independent random seeds, producing a dataset of realized values (or signals), bidder actions, allocations, prices, profits, and free-text plans. Here, ``actions'' refers to: a numeric bid in sealed-bid formats, a \texttt{stay}/\texttt{exit} decision at each clock tick in ascending auctions, and an \texttt{increase}/\texttt{hold} decision over a bidder's maximum bid in the eBay proxy-bidding environment.

\paragraph{Single-auction baseline and learning ablation.}
Our main benchmark experiments use \emph{single-auction} simulations: each repetition $k$ is an independent auction instance with freshly drawn values/signals, and bidders do not receive feedback across instances. We adopt this design to minimize reliance on cross-instance learning and long-term adaptation, which remains unreliable for language models \citep{dong2024survey}. This choice is distinct from whether a mechanism is \emph{iterative} within an auction (e.g., clock auctions): iterative mechanisms still unfold over multiple within-auction steps (clock ticks), but we do not run repeated auctions with feedback by default. In Appendix~\ref{app:learning}, we run a repeated-auction ablation for sealed-bid auctions in which bidders observe outcomes from prior auction instances; consistent with the motivation above, we find no meaningful learning effects across repetitions.

\paragraph{Value models and numerical parameters.}
We implement three canonical information structures. All parameters (support ranges, bidder count $n$, and bid/price increments) are specified in configuration files and held fixed within a setting. In sealed-bid auctions, bids are restricted to a discrete grid with increment $0.1$; in ascending clock auctions, the clock price increases by increment $0.5$ per tick.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Independent Private Values (IPV).} Each bidder's value is the sum of an independent private component and (optionally) a common component. In our baseline IPV parameterization, the private component from $[0,49]$, yielding
    \[
          \epsilon_{it} \sim \mathrm{Unif}[0,49], \qquad v_{it}=\epsilon_{it}.
    \]
    We discretize bids with increment $0.1$.

    \item \textbf{Affiliated Private Values (APV).} Values share a common component plus an idiosyncratic private shock. Our APV parameterization sets
    \[
        c_t \sim \mathrm{Unif}[0,29], \qquad \epsilon_{it} \sim \mathrm{Unif}[0,20], \qquad v_{it}=c_t+\epsilon_{it},
    \]
    again with bid increment $0.1$. This induces affiliation: higher realized values for one bidder make higher values for others more likely.

    \item \textbf{Common Value (CV).} The item has an underlying common value $V_t$ shared by all bidders, while each bidder observes a noisy private signal. In our CV parameterization, the common value is drawn from $[20,29]$ and each bidder observes an idiosyncratically perturbed signal,
    \[
        V_t \sim \mathrm{Unif}[20,29], \qquad \eta_{it} \sim \mathrm{Unif}[-20,20], \qquad s_{it}=V_t+\eta_{it},
    \]
    with bid increment $0.1$. Bidder $i$ conditions on $s_{it}$ when bidding, while realized payoffs depend on $V_t$, generating the winner's-curse selection effect.
\end{itemize}
In our baseline benchmark, $T=1$ so each repetition corresponds to a single auction instance.

\paragraph{Bidder instructions.}
Every simulated bidder is assigned a value (or signal) and receives the same instruction to maximize expected profit. This choice follows the design of the human experiments we benchmark against. Laboratory auctions are incentivized: subjects' earnings rise with their auction payoffs, and the instructions encourage them to earn as much as they can \citep{smith1976experimental, smith1982microeconomic}. The experiments of \citet{kagel1993independent}, \citet{li2017obviously}, \citet{breitmoser2022obviousness}, and \citet{gonczarowski2023strategyproofness} all induce values in this way and pay subjects accordingly. Within each model and setting, bidders share the same objective and differ in their assigned values or signals. Human subjects additionally differ in preferences, experience, and cognition, even under the same induced-value instructions; we do not assign demographic or personality descriptions, which is one reason to expect magnitudes to differ (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). In the one-shot baseline the objective instruction reads: ``You have only one opportunity to bid. Your top priority is to place a bid that maximizes your expected profit.''; a multi-round variant is used only in the learning ablation (Appendix~\ref{app:learning}).

\subsubsection{Canonical laboratory mechanisms.}
To mirror standard laboratory benchmarks, we implement \textit{sealed-bid} auctions and \textit{ascending clock} auctions under the value models above.

\paragraph{Sealed-bid auctions.}
In sealed-bid auctions, each bidder submits a single bid $b_{it}$ simultaneously. Let $b_{(1)t}\ge b_{(2)t}\ge b_{(3)t}$ denote the order statistics and let $i^\star_t$ be the highest bidder. The winner pays a price $p_t$ determined by the mechanism:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{First-price sealed-bid (FP).} $i^\star_t$ wins and pays $p_t=b_{(1)t}$.
    \item \textbf{Second-price sealed-bid (SP).} $i^\star_t$ wins and pays $p_t=b_{(2)t}$.
\end{itemize}
Profits are computed as $\pi_{it}=v_{it}-p_t$ for winners (or $\pi_{it}=V_t-p_t$ in CV) and $0$ for losers.

\paragraph{Ascending clock auctions.}
In an ascending clock auction, the price starts at 0 and increases by a fixed increment $\Delta_{\text{clock}}=0.5$ at each clock tick. At each tick, each bidder chooses \emph{stay} or \emph{exit}. The last remaining bidder wins. We implement the standard second-price logic in the dynamic interface: the winner pays the exit price of the second-to-last bidder.

\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{One LLM bidder per clock auction.}\label{sec:one_llm_clock}
In the ascending clock auctions, one of the three bidders is an LLM and the other two are programmed to play the dominant strategy: each stays in until the price reaches its value and then exits. The LLM receives the same prompt as in the sealed-bid and earlier clock experiments (Appendix~\ref{app:dynamic}) and is not told that its opponents are programmed. Fixing the opponents' behavior means that every LLM decision is taken against the same environment, and it lets us record the LLM's own exit price $e_i$, the clock analogue of a bid. In the open clock (AC), bidders observe dropouts and the auction ends when the second-to-last bidder exits; when the LLM is the last bidder remaining, its exit price is never observed and the auction is censored. In the closed clock (AC-B), bidders do not observe dropouts, so the clock keeps running for the LLM after both opponents have exited until the LLM exits; allocation and price are those of the standard closed clock, and the LLM's exit price is observed in every auction. The SMAD of a clock cell is $100\cdot\overline{|e_i-v_i|}/25$ over the uncensored exits, on the same scale as the sealed-bid formats. Each cell has 50 single-round auctions with independent seeds, and failed API calls are retried up to eight times with backoff. Because censoring removes the open-clock auctions the LLM wins, we add batches of 15 auctions with new seeds until each open-clock cell has at least 30 uncensored exits. Appendix~\ref{app:data_inventory} reports, for every cell, the auctions attempted, completed, and used. Exit prices lie on the $0.5$ price grid, so an exit one increment from value corresponds to an SMAD of $2\%$, and smaller differences between clock cells are within one tick.
\end{revblock}


\paragraph{Clock-framing intervention (mechanism held fixed).}
To isolate the effect of description while holding auction design constant, we implement an alternative framing of the second-price sealed-bid auction:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clock framing.} Bidders are told to treat their sealed bid as an automatic exit threshold in a simulated ascending clock: conceptually, the clock rises until bidders ``drop out'' at their thresholds, and the winner pays the second-highest threshold.
\end{itemize}
The framing implements the same allocation and payment rule as the second-price sealed-bid auction; only the prompt language differs. The precise prompts are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:prompt}.

The eBay-style proxy-bidding environment used in Section~\ref{session:ebay} is a separate, marketplace-inspired environment; its rules, treatments, and discrete-time implementation are described in Appendix~\ref{app:ebay_env}.

\subsubsection{Simulation protocol.}
Each experiment proceeds as follows, repeated for $K$ independent repetitions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Value realization.} For each repetition $k$, we draw values (IPV/APV) or signals (CV) for all bidders under a fixed random seed. In our baseline benchmark experiments, each repetition corresponds to a single auction instance.
    \item \textbf{Prompted action.} We construct an individualized prompt for each bidder containing (i) the mechanism rules (and framing, if applicable), (ii) the bidder's current value (or signal), and (iii) when applicable, a feedback transcript summarizing prior auction instances (only in the learning ablation). We request a structured response with explicit tags:
\begin{quote}\small
\verb|<PLAN> ... </PLAN>| \\
\verb|<ACTION> ... </ACTION>|
\end{quote}
where \verb|<ACTION>| contains a numeric bid (sealed-bid), a stay/exit decision (clock), or an increase/hold decision over the bidder's maximum bid (eBay).
    \item \textbf{Parallel elicitation and parsing.} We query the LLM for each bidder using separate API calls in parallel, and parse the action from the \verb|<ACTION>| tag using a deterministic regex-based extractor.
    \item \textbf{Outcome computation.} Given the submitted actions, we determine the winner and payments according to the mechanism rules and compute bidder profits.
\end{enumerate}

Unless otherwise noted, our baseline settings use $n=3$ bidders and we repeat each configuration $K$ times with independent seeds to ensure stable distributional estimates.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Three Tiers of Comparison}{Three Tiers of Comparison}}\label{sec:tiers}

We compare LLM and human behavior at three tiers.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Magnitudes.} The size of SMAD. Because the human anchors are reconstructions, magnitude comparisons are qualitative, and we ask whether a model's SMAD falls within the human reconstruction band.
  \item \emph{Directions.} After removing bids within $\pm 2\%$ of the benchmark, $|b-b^\star| \le 0.02\, b^\star$, we classify the remaining bids as underbids or overbids and compare the shares. Human direction shares use the source studies' reported categories in the truthful formats and the reconstruction in first-price auctions.
  \item \emph{Orderings.} We rank formats by SMAD and compute Kendall's $\tau_b$ between the human ranking and each model's ranking over the five private-value formats that share a common benchmark scale (FPSB IPV, SPSB IPV, SP-APV, AC, AC-B), with uncertainty from two layers: resampling LLM bids within each cell, and re-drawing the human reconstruction within its admissible ranges (Appendix~\ref{app:concordance}). For design contrasts we ask whether the \emph{sign} of the human effect (first-price harder than second-price; clock easier than sealed bid) is reproduced.
\end{itemize}
Effect sizes and error directions matter for design objectives such as revenue and efficiency. Ordinal agreement can inform comparisons between mechanisms, and it is the tier at which we claim LLM bidders are informative.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Model Panel}{Model Panel}}\label{sec:models}

To run the simulation, we evaluate five LLMs under identical prompts. We choose three popular foundation models from different providers, OpenAI's gpt-4o (2024-08-06), Anthropic's claude-3-5-haiku (2024-10-22), and Google's gemini-2.0-flash (2025-02-05), which we call the non-reasoning large model family; one reasoning model, gpt-5-mini (2025-08-07), which allocates extended test-time computation to each decision; and one widely used open-source model, Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct. All models are prompted with the instrument described in Section~\ref{sec:data_generation}. Each model is run in separate auctions with bidders using that model; the panel compares results across models rather than mixing models within an auction. Section~\ref{sec:benchmark_experimental} focuses on the three non-reasoning large models and reports the reasoning and small models as comparisons. GPT-4o is in addition the reference model for the deeper distributional diagnostics (temperature ablation, repeated rounds, and the eBay-style environment of Section~\ref{session:ebay}) and therefore has the largest cells. For non-reasoning models we set the temperature to $0.5$, the most common setting in LLM simulation \citep{horton2023large, Horton2024EDSL}; for gpt-5-mini the API ignores temperature. For GPT-4o we also ablate $\text{temp}\in\{0.1, 1.0\}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:ablation}). \rev{Three of these models are no longer served by our inference provider (checked in November 2026), so the clock cells, which use the one-LLM design of Section~\ref{sec:one_llm_clock}, use the closest current model of the same family: claude-haiku-5-5 for Claude~3.5 Haiku, gemini-2.5-flash with its thinking budget set to zero for Gemini~2.0 Flash, and Meta-Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct for Llama-3-8B. The GPT-4o and GPT-5-mini clock cells use the same model as their sealed-bid cells.}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Tier 1: Magnitudes do not match}{Tier 1: Magnitudes do not match}}\label{sec:tier_magnitudes}

Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison} compares SMAD magnitudes. Against the human reconstruction bands of Appendix~\ref{app:bands}, the reference model GPT-4o falls inside the band in one of the five private-value formats (SP-APV, $10.7\%$ vs.\ $[6.8, 11.7]$); it deviates more than humans in the sealed-bid formats ($27.6\%$ vs.\ $24.8\%$ in first-price, $8.7\%$ vs.\ $5.6\%$ in second-price) and far less in the clock formats (\rev{\SmadACGpt\%} vs.\ $3.5\%$ in the open clock, \rev{\SmadACBGpt\%} vs.\ \rev{$5.5\%$} in the closed clock). In the common-value formats its deviations are $26\%$ and $123\%$ larger than the human anchors. The other models do no better: Claude~3.5 Haiku and Gemini~2.0 Flash fall inside the human band in two of five private-value formats each, GPT-5-mini in none (its SMAD is below $1.4\%$ in every private-value format\rev{, clocks included}, essentially the theoretical benchmark), and Llama-3-8B in none (its SMAD exceeds $38\%$ \rev{in every sealed private-value format and is \SmadACLlama\% and \SmadACBLlama\% in the open and closed clocks}). Even where a single magnitude coincides, as with Claude's first-price SMAD of $24.1\%$ against the human $24.8\%$, the same model is off by a factor of two in the adjacent second-price format. We conclude that LLM simulations should not be read for the magnitude of human deviations, and the rest of this section therefore turns to the two qualitative tiers.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Tier 2: Directions do not match}{Tier 2: Directions do not match}}\label{sec:tier_directions}

Figure~\ref{fig:overbidding} compares the direction of bid errors\rev{, splitting each bidder's bids into those below, within $\pm 2\%$ of, and above the theoretical benchmark. The shares quoted in the text are among deviating bids, those outside the band (Table~\ref{tab:direction_models}).} Human shares use the source studies' reported categories in the truthful formats and the reconstructed bids in first-price auctions, so their tolerance convention need not match the LLM convention.

In first-price IPV auctions, humans and the non-reasoning large models all predominantly overbid relative to the equilibrium shading rule. Overbids account for $67.5\%$ of human deviating bids, after removing $6.4\%$ near-equilibrium bids, and for $85.9\%$, $97.8\%$, and $89.9\%$ of the deviating bids of GPT-4o, Claude~3.5 Haiku, and Gemini~2.0 Flash, after removing $3.2\%$, $1.1\%$, and $1.1\%$. The small model underbids ($82\%$ of Llama-3-8B's deviating bids), and the reasoning model has no deviating bids: every GPT-5-mini bid lies within the tolerance band around the shading rule. Second-price IPV reverses the agreement. \citet{kagel1993independent} report $67\%$ of human bids above value, $27\%$ at value, and $6\%$ below, implying that about $92\%$ of non-truthful bids are overbids. Among GPT-4o's deviating bids, $81.3\%$ are underbids, and $30.7\%$ of its bids lie within the tolerance band; Gemini~2.0 Flash underbids in all of its deviating bids, whereas Claude~3.5 Haiku overbids in roughly two thirds of its deviating bids, the one member of the family that errs in the human direction. The small model also predominantly underbids ($94\%$), and the reasoning model has a single deviating bid.

In sealed-bid SP-APV, the aggregate deviations are similar for humans and GPT-4o ($9.3\%$ and $10.7\%$), but the errors have opposite directions: \rev{$64\%$} of human deviating bids are overbids, while $92.4\%$ of GPT-4o's are underbids, as are $70\%$ of Claude~3.5 Haiku's and all of Gemini~2.0 Flash's. All five models predominantly underbid in this setting. \rev{The clock formats carry little directional information for the LLMs: \TickBothGpt\ of GPT-4o's open- and closed-clock exits are within one price increment of value, so only \NdevACGpt\ and \NdevACBGpt\ of its exits fall outside the $\pm 2\%$ band, and their direction mostly reflects on which side of its value the model exits on the price grid.} Directional agreement is thus distinct from agreement in either the average error or the full bid distribution.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Payment rule: first-price is harder than second-price}{Payment rule: first-price is harder than second-price}}

We first test whether LLM bidders adjust appropriately when the payment rule changes from first-price to second-price sealed-bid auctions, a particularly clean economic environment because the allocation rule is unchanged, while the mapping from a bid to the winning payment differs sharply. In the IPV environment, Bayes--Nash equilibrium predicts systematic bid shading in the first-price auction, whereas in the second-price auction truthful bidding is a dominant strategy. Human bidders find the first-price format far harder (SMAD $24.8\%$ against $5.6\%$, a $4.4\times$ gap; \citealp{kagel1993independent}). The non-reasoning large models reproduce the ordering: first-price SMAD exceeds second-price SMAD for GPT-4o ($27.6\%$ vs.\ $8.7\%$), Claude~3.5 Haiku ($24.1\%$ vs.\ $9.7\%$), and Gemini~2.0 Flash ($27.1\%$ vs.\ $3.0\%$), with gaps between $2.5\times$ and $9\times$ around the human $4.4\times$. The reasoning model makes the ordering uninformative: GPT-5-mini bids on the equilibrium shading rule in the first-price auction ($0.1\%$) and at value in the second-price auction ($0.2\%$), leaving nothing to order. The small model inverts it: Llama-3-8B deviates more in the second-price auction ($54.9\%$) than in the first-price auction ($38.2\%$).

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Implementation: clock beats sealed bid}{Implementation: clock beats sealed bid}}

Besides the payment rule, another important variation in the economic environment relates to the \emph{interaction structure}, i.e., whether it is a one-shot sealed-bid auction or an iterative bidding process in a clock style. Clock auctions are dynamic formats where the price increases incrementally and bidders decide whether to stay in or drop out at each price point. For second-price auctions, although the sealed-bid format and the ascending-clock format share the same dominant strategy, human participants behave quite differently. A robust pattern in the experimental literature is that bidders do much better in clock-style auctions than in sealed-bid auctions. \citet{li2017obviously} provides an argument as to why clock auctions may be easier to recognize as strategy-proof for humans, and formalizes this as an equilibrium refinement on strategy-proofness called {\em obvious strategy-proofness (OSP)}. \citet{breitmoser2022obviousness} provide additional evidence that clock auctions improve truthful play. In the affiliated private values (APV) environment, human SMAD is $3.5\%$ in the open clock (AC) and \rev{$5.5\%$} in the closed clock (AC-B), compared to $9.3\%$ in the sealed bid. This ranking reinforces the standard interpretation that dynamic, incremental ``stay/exit'' decisions lower cognitive load relative to mapping a value into a single, one-shot bid. \rev{The non-reasoning large models reproduce the ordering. GPT-4o's SMAD falls from $10.7\%$ in the sealed bid to \SmadACGpt\% in the open clock and \SmadACBGpt\% in the closed clock, a larger clock advantage than the human one; \TickBothGpt\ of its exits are within one price increment of value. The clock cells of Claude and Gemini, run with replacement models (Section~\ref{sec:models}), show the same pattern: Claude's SMAD falls from $9.6\%$ in the sealed bid to \SmadACClaude\% and \SmadACBClaude\% in the open and closed clocks, and Gemini's from $8.0\%$ to \SmadACGemini\% and \SmadACBGemini\%. All three models deviate less in the clocks than humans do, so they reproduce the sign of the clock advantage but overstate its size. The reasoning model again offers no ordering to test: GPT-5-mini is at the benchmark in the sealed bid ($1.4\%$) and in the clocks (\SmadACMini\% and \SmadACBMini\%), and the differences are within one price increment. The small model reproduces the sign but not the level: Llama-3-8B's clock SMADs (\SmadACLlama\% and \SmadACBLlama\%, replacement model) are below its sealed-bid SMAD ($53.1\%$) but several times the human values, and only \TickACLlama\% and \TickACBLlama\% of its exits are within one increment of value.} On the human side, the open-clock ordering holds in $100\%$ of reconstruction re-draws and the closed-clock ordering in \rev{$96.5\%$}, reflecting the wide standard error reported for the closed clock \citep{breitmoser2022obviousness}; we treat the closed-clock ordering as the less secure of the two.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Information structure: common value and competition}{Information structure: common value and competition}}

Common-value auctions provide a particularly sharp test of whether bidders correctly condition on the adverse selection implicit in winning. In the canonical experimental design of \citet{kagel1986winner}, each bidder observes a noisy private signal about a common underlying value. Winning is therefore ``bad news'': conditional on having the highest signal, the expected common value is below the winner's signal. If bidders neglect (or incompletely correct for) this conditioning, they overbid and the winner earns negative profits---the classic \emph{winner's curse}. \rev{In human experiments the curse is severe in first-price sealed-bid auctions \citep{kagel1986winner} and much weaker in English (open ascending) auctions, where bidders observe rivals' drop-out prices \citep{levin1996revenue}. Our human second-price common-value anchor is this English auction, which shares the second-price payment rule but also reveals information (Table~\ref{tab:human_sources}), so the human ordering compares first-price sealed bids with English auctions.} Because the benchmark for common-value bids is profit-based, we compare SMAD only within the information structure. All three model classes reproduce the ordering. First-price common-value deviations exceed second-price deviations for the non-reasoning large models (GPT-4o $60.0\%$ vs.\ $40.6\%$, Claude~3.5 Haiku $54.1\%$ vs.\ $27.2\%$, Gemini~2.0 Flash $59.5\%$ vs.\ $30.9\%$), for the reasoning model (GPT-5-mini $30.2\%$ vs.\ $14.8\%$), and for the small model (Llama-3-8B $53.7\%$ vs.\ $32.3\%$), as they do for humans ($47.6\%$ vs.\ $18.2\%$). This ordering is preserved by both comparison models; unlike in the private-value settings, GPT-5-mini makes substantial errors in common-value auctions. Appendix~\ref{app:winners_curse} reports, for the reference model, how winner losses vary with the number of bidders.


\section{\texorpdfstring{Application to Design: Interventions and Market Structure}{Application to Design: Interventions and Market Structure}}\label{session:ebay}

The laboratory benchmarks of Section~\ref{sec:benchmark_experimental} establish where LLM bidders agree with human bidders: at the ordinal tier, for the non-reasoning large model family. This section asks whether that agreement is useful for design. Before running a human experiment, a designer typically wants to know two things: whether a proposed intervention is likely to help, and whether one market structure is likely to induce better behavior than another. We use the panel to answer both. First, we treat the ordinal result as a prediction: a presentation change that improves human play should improve play for the non-reasoning large models, and we test one such change, the clock framing of a sealed-bid second-price auction \citep{breitmoser2022obviousness}. Second, we move from stylized laboratory mechanisms to a market structure documented in field data, an eBay-style proxy-bidding marketplace, and ask whether the panel recovers the ordering of bidding dynamics across closing rules.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Clock framing as an intervention: a prediction test}{Clock framing as an intervention: a prediction test}}\label{session:Intervention}

Recent studies consider whether behavior in strategy-proof auctions depends on how the mechanism is \emph{presented}, holding the underlying incentives fixed. \citet{breitmoser2022obviousness} show that \emph{reframing} a second-price sealed-bid (SPSB) auction as a dynamic ascending clock substantially increases truth-telling among human subjects, consistent with the idea that step-by-step ``stay/exit'' choices reduce the cognitive burden of mapping a value into a single sealed bid.\footnote{\citet{gonczarowski2023strategyproofness} study a second presentation change, an extensive-form \emph{menu} description of the same auction, and find no reliable effect on human bidding. We do not use it here because a null human effect makes no ordinal prediction.} If the non-reasoning large models preserve the ordering of human difficulty, the clock framing should raise their truthful play as well; the reasoning model, already at the benchmark, offers no room for improvement. We implement the clock framing as a change of prompt only: the allocation and payment rule are unchanged (Section~\ref{sec:data_generation}; prompts in Appendix~\ref{app:intervention}). Across all conditions we summarize behavior by the per-bid scaled deviation $(b-v)/\mathbb{E}[b^\star]$, with the same scaling as SMAD; negative values are underbids and positive values overbids.\footnote{The GPT-4o cells are those of Table~\ref{tab:clock_framing_main}, generated with the same templates as the other models.}

For the reference model the prediction holds. Under the traditional description, GPT-4o bids are systematically below value (mean deviation $-0.118$, s.d.\ $0.128$, $n=300$). Under the clock framing the mean deviation moves to $-0.062$ (a $47\%$ reduction in average underbidding), the dispersion falls from $0.128$ to $0.084$, and the share of bids within $\pm 2\%$ of value rises from $28\%$ to $45\%$; both a Welch $t$-test and a Mann--Whitney test reject equality at $p<10^{-5}$ (Cohen's $d=0.48$). Table~\ref{tab:clock_framing_main} extends the test to the other two non-reasoning large model families, run with the same templates. The clock framing lowers SMAD for Claude~3.5 Haiku ($8.1\to7.1\%$) as it does for GPT-4o ($11.8\to6.2\%$), and raises it for Gemini~2.0 Flash ($5.0\to9.3\%$). The exception is instructive: Gemini's baseline second-price play is already the closest to truthful of the three ($62\%$ of bids within $\pm 2\%$ of value), so it is the family nearest the ceiling in this format, where the ordinal argument predicts no gain. Appendix~\ref{app:clock_robustness} repeats the test for an open-weights model outside the main panel, Gemma-3-27B, whose SMAD falls from $26.3\%$ to $13.5\%$. Claude's case is also worth reading closely: its mean absolute deviation falls only slightly, while the share of exactly truthful bids drops from $28\%$ to $7\%$. The clock framing removes Claude's overbidding in the human direction and replaces it with mild, uniform underbidding, a change in direction more than in magnitude. The prediction that a lever which helps human bidders helps the non-reasoning large models is thus borne out for the models with non-degenerate baseline error and fails, as it should, where there is no error to remove.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Market structure: closing rules in an eBay-style marketplace}{Market structure: closing rules in an eBay-style marketplace}}\label{sec:ebay_market}


Having tested LLM behavior against human behavior in controlled laboratory experiment settings, we study a more complicated auction setting modeled after eBay's  auction-based marketplace. eBay's auction platform, studied by \citet{einav2011learning} and others, features various design elements---for example, proxy bidding and a hard closing time---that have been shown empirically to have substantial effects on bidder behavior. These differences highlight the gap between \textit{lab experiment} and \textit{real-world bidding}, where seemingly small design details can induce large shifts in timing, competition, and price discovery.

We implement a simplified eBay-style environment that isolates one design parameter documented in the empirical literature: the auction \emph{closing rule}. The core mechanics of an eBay auction are simple: bidders on eBay use a proxy bidding system, which means that each bidder submits their ``maximum bid" to the platform, and the platform automatically bids on their behalf, only as much as necessary to keep the winning bidder in the lead (i.e., the second-highest bid + \$1, with incremental bidding managed automatically).
Bidders can increase their maximum bid at any point in time, until some closing time of
the auction, giving this the flavor
of an open, second-price auction.
As described in Appendix~\ref{app:ebay_env}, we vary the closing rule to obtain two treatments: (T1) standard eBay proxy bidding with a hard close, and (T2) proxy bidding with a soft-close (auction-extension) rule, in the spirit of early Amazon auctions. We ask whether LLM bidders recover the \emph{ordering} of bidding dynamics across the two closing rules that the field evidence documents: more last-moment bidding under a hard close than under a soft close.


\paragraph{LLMs exhibit bid sniping under hard-close rules, and respond to soft-close extensions.}\label{sec:ebay_sniping}


A hallmark of hard-close online auctions is \emph{bid sniping}: bidders delay their bids until the final moments of the auction to avoid triggering competitive responses . Sniping is widely observed on eBay \footnote{For example: \url{https://www.esnipe.com/}.}, and has long been studied as a behavioral and strategic departure from the simple ``bid-your-value'' logic implied by proxy bidding \citep{roth2002last}.
From the standpoint of market design, sniping is potentially costly because it compresses information revelation into the final moments of the auction, weakening timely price discovery.
A classic intervention is a \emph{soft-close} (auction-extension) rule: if a new maximum bid arrives near the end, the auction is extended, allowing additional responses. Empirically, this design detail is credited with substantial differences in bidding dynamics between early Amazon.com auctions (soft close) and eBay (hard close).
Specifically, \citet{roth2002last} and \citet{ockenfels2006late} show that much less sniping occurred on Amazon.com, with bids on Amazon.com occurring throughout the auction compared to the empirical evidence of considerable bid
sniping on eBay.


We ask whether LLM bidders respond to these temporal incentives in the same direction. We measure the \emph{final-winning-bid time}, defined as the last period in which the eventual winner changes her maximum bid. Intuitively, this statistic captures when the winner is effectively determined. Figure~\ref{fig:final_winning_bid_shift} reports histograms of this timing under hard close versus soft close, and Appendix~\ref{app:ebay_extra} reports the corresponding cumulative distributions.

Two patterns emerge. First, under the standard hard-close rule (T1), LLM bidders concentrate their decisive bid updates near the end of the auction, consistent with sniping behavior: in $63\%$ of hard-close auctions ($19$ of $30$) the final winning bid is placed in the last scheduled period, and the median final-winning-bid period is the last one. Second, introducing a soft-close extension rule (T2) sharply attenuates this last-period concentration: the distribution of final-winning-bid times shifts earlier, and last-period decisive bids become substantially less frequent: $7\%$ of soft-close auctions ($2$ of $30$) are decided in the last scheduled period, one more in an extension period, and the median final-winning-bid period falls to $5.5$ (Mann--Whitney $p=0.0002$; Kolmogorov--Smirnov $p=0.0003$). In short, LLM bidders respond strongly to the closing rule in the same qualitative direction emphasized in the human empirical literature: soft close de-incentivizes waiting and spreads competition earlier in time, improving the timing of information revelation. There is no human anchor for the timing distribution in our stylized environment, so we compare only the ordering of the two closing rules, and it matches the field evidence, as the ordinal reading of Section~\ref{sec:benchmark_experimental} predicts.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{What LLM simulation can and cannot deliver for mechanism design}{What LLM simulation can and cannot deliver for mechanism design}}

Simulations are proliferating in high-stakes engineering disciplines, where iterative design and validation are performed long before prototypes are built. In contrast, traditional mechanism design research often relies on expensive, time-intensive human subject experiments or confounded observational data. Our synthetic lab uses the rapidly advancing capabilities of large language models to simulate bidder behavior at a fraction of the cost, and our results locate the role that such simulation can play. In magnitude, LLM behavior is not human behavior, and in direction it is often the opposite of human behavior: the same second-price auction that induces overbidding in the laboratory induces underbidding in most models. Any use of LLM bidders that reads off magnitudes (predicted revenue, predicted efficiency loss, predicted overbidding rates) is not supported by our evidence. At the ordinal level, however, the picture is different. For the non-reasoning large models, whose baseline deviations are neither negligible nor overwhelming, the cross-mechanism ordering of difficulty, the sign of the clock advantage, the sign of the first- versus second-price common-value comparison, the sign of the clock-framing effect, and the ordering of last-minute bidding across closing rules all match the human evidence. These are exactly the questions a designer asks when choosing among candidate mechanisms or presentations, and they are questions that dominant-strategy theory alone cannot answer, since it is silent on how the same incentives presented differently will be played.

We therefore propose that LLM bidders be used as an \emph{ordinal screening} instrument within a two-tier workflow. In the first tier, a panel of models is run on the candidate designs, and the designs are ranked, or the sign of a proposed change is estimated, at a cost of a few dollars per cell. In the second tier, the designs that survive screening are taken to a human experiment, whose purpose is to establish magnitudes and directions, which the panel cannot supply. Three practices follow from our results. Models should be checked against known human orderings before they are used to screen new designs: neither nearly exact equilibrium play nor large errors alone establish suitability. Each model's use of extended reasoning and observed baseline errors should be reported with its results. And agreement across the panel, rather than the behavior of any single model, should be the evidence that an ordering is stable.

LLM agents operate on a text-based reasoning framework that captures nuanced decision-making processes. This also affords the opportunity to query these LLM agents about their internal deliberations, opening up the prospect, with further study and validation, that LLMs may also provide information on the human cognitive constraints that underlie deviations from classical auction theory.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Practical advantages and the scope for mechanism design}{Practical advantages and the scope for mechanism design}}

Classical lab experiments in auction settings have yielded valuable insights, but also come with high cost and logistical challenges. LLM experiments avoid participant recruitment and can be repeated while varying a prompt or an auction rule. The analysis of observational datasets from platforms like eBay is often challenging because one needs to control for the heterogeneity among sellers and buyers, complicating causal inference. In our synthetic lab, we can isolate individual auction parameters and rule framings by carefully controlling the environment in which bids are generated, including the simulated goods and valuations of bidders \citep{manning2024automated}. The rise of online human labs \citep{horton2011online} in behavioral economics has previously lowered the barrier to performing controlled experiments \citep{oprea2024decisions, enke2024behavioral}, enabling near real-time data collection and hypothesis testing. Our synthetic lab extends this idea by eliminating many of the ethical, logistical, and opportunity-cost issues associated with human experiments. Whether the effects identified in simulation carry over to humans requires separate validation. The low API cost of the reported experiments (under \$400 for the GPT-4o experiments, \rev{against the recruitment and subject payments of a comparable laboratory study such as \citealp{li2017obviously}}) is therefore a practical advantage for exploration, not evidence that they replace human experiments.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Limitations}{Limitations}}

Two limitations bound the interpretation. First, the human benchmarks are reconstructions of published moments, not micro data. The bands we report measure how far an anchor can move within what the source statistics leave undetermined; they are not sampling bands on human behavior, and we accordingly use the reconstructions only for orderings, directions, and comparative statics. Second, the variation across simulated bidders is confined to their induced values: every bidder receives the same objective and the same motivation. This mirrors the induced-value design of the human experiments we benchmark against, but it leaves open how a panel would behave if bidders also varied along other dimensions, such as their attitude to risk. Adding such variation in ways that preserve the incentive structure of the human experiments is a natural extension.


\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper reports the results of more than 1,000 auction experiments with LLM bidders across five models, benchmarked against human laboratory evidence that we made comparable across mechanisms by reconstructing published moments. LLM bidders do not replicate human auction behavior: their bid distributions differ in magnitude, and in the second-price auction most models err in the opposite direction from human subjects. Without task-specific fine-tuning or calibration to human bids, however, the three non-reasoning large models preserve the main orderings of auction difficulty. Clock implementations reduce deviations wherever data are adequate, and clock framing helps two of the three models. More accurate equilibrium play does not guarantee greater similarity to humans: the reasoning model's near-equilibrium private-value bids leave little variation in errors to compare, while the small model's large errors yield an inverted ranking. Both nevertheless preserve the first- versus second-price common-value ordering.

Auctions thus illustrate a broader research agenda for LLM simulations of human behavior: determine which features of behavior a model preserves, distinguishing magnitudes, directions, and orderings. Our results establish an out-of-the-box baseline for this agenda, complementary to models specifically trained on human choices. Where orderings have been validated, an LLM panel may help screen candidate interventions before human experiments establish their effects. We see the main contribution of this work as putting forward a framework for how to think about LLM experimental agents as a proxy for human agents in a particular comparison. While this paper focuses on auction design, future work may use LLM sandboxes to test other kinds of economic mechanisms, e.g., voting, matching, and contract design. In particular, some auction formats, such as combinatorial auctions, are complex and difficult to run frequently and at scale in traditional lab experiments. Augmenting these traditional lab experiments with LLM-agent experiments, when correctly validated, may open up wide new avenues to better understand the design tradeoffs in these kinds of complex and often high-stakes environments.

\subsection{Benchmark Records and Mappings}\label{app:human_records}
This section lists, for each row of Table~\ref{tab:human_sources}, the treatment in the original article, the printed statistics we use, and the computation that turns them into $\Delta_m$. All inputs are published summary statistics; we use no individual records.

\paragraph{Kagel and Levin (1993): first- and second-price IPV.}
The experiment runs sealed-bid auctions with independent private values drawn uniformly, with $N=5$ and $N=10$ bidders, in inexperienced and experienced series \citep{kagel1993independent}. We use the $N=5$ series, pooling the inexperienced and experienced rows with equal weights. For each payment rule the article reports a bid-on-value regression and the shares of bids below, at, and above value (Table~2). The inputs are $(\hat\alpha,\hat\beta,R^2)=(1.14,0.92,0.88)$ and shares $(92.1,7.5,0.4)\%$ for the first-price auction, and $(0.23,1.08,0.97)$ and $(5.7,27.0,67.2)\%$ for the second-price auction. These regression statistics and shares cannot be matched jointly by one parametric model (Section~\ref{sec:kl93_conflict}). We therefore set the reconstruction's parameters: bids are simulated for $x\sim U[x_{\min},x_{\max}]$ on the article's value support with a mean bid-to-value ratio given by the regression and direction weights given by the reported shares, the remaining shape parameters are fixed as in Table~\ref{tab:mixture_fit}, and we compute $\Delta_m=100\cdot\mathbb{E}|b-b^\star(x)|/\mathbb{E}[b^\star(x)]$ with $b^\star=\tfrac45 x$ (first price) and $b^\star=x$ (second price). The shares ``at value'' measure value bidding; in the first-price auction they are not equilibrium bidding. The third-price auction (Table~2; inputs $(-0.09,1.25,0.94)$ and $(4.2,6.1,89.8)\%$, $b^\star=\tfrac43x$) is treated the same way and used only in the supplementary third-price comparison. Code: \texttt{analysis/moment\_matching\_kagel\_levin\_1993.py}.

\paragraph{Li (2017): sealed second price and open ascending clock, APV.}
\citet{li2017obviously} compares a sealed second-price auction (2P) with an ascending clock (AC) under affiliated private values, and reports the mean absolute difference between the second-highest bid and the second-highest value in each group, with standard errors, separately for rounds 1--5 and rounds 6--10 (Table~3, auction rows). The printed values are $8.04$ (SE $1.25$) and $4.99$ (SE $1.18$) for 2P, and $3.19$ (SE $1.05$) and $1.77$ (SE $0.33$) for AC. We average the two blocks and combine their standard errors as independent, which gives $\mathrm{MAD}=6.52$ (SE 0.86) for 2P and $\mathrm{MAD}=2.48$ (SE 0.55) for AC. Because truthful bidding is the benchmark in both formats, $\Delta_m=100\cdot\mathrm{MAD}_m/\mathbb{E}[v]$ and $\mathrm{SE}(\Delta_m)=100\cdot\mathrm{SE}(\mathrm{MAD}_m)/\mathbb{E}[v]$. A value is the sum of a group draw, uniform on $[10,110]$, and a private adjustment, uniform on $[0,20]$, so $\mathbb{E}[v]=70$. Li's statistic is defined at the group level from the second-highest bid and value; we use it as printed. \rev{The direction weights come from Li's Figure~2(a), a histogram of the same group-level difference: $22\%$, $39\%$, and $39\%$ of groups fall below $-\$2$, within $\pm\$2$, and above $\$2$ in 2P, and $11\%$, $70\%$, and $19\%$ in AC.}

\paragraph{Breitmoser and Schweighofer-Kodritsch (2022): ascending clock without dropout information.}
\citet{breitmoser2022obviousness} varies how the second-price mechanism is presented. Our AC-B benchmark uses their treatment AC-B (``Ascending Clock -- Blind''), an ascending clock in which bidders do not observe their opponents dropping out. The study reports the mean absolute deviation of bids from values by treatment and round, and decomposes its reduction from the sealed bid to the clock into the contributions of clock presentation, dynamic bidding, and dropout information (Figures~1 and~2 of the working-paper version \citep{breitmoser2019obviousness}). The mapping is the same as for Li, $\Delta=100\cdot\mathrm{MAD}/\mathbb{E}[v]$, and the value distribution is also the same, so $\mathbb{E}[v]=70$. Appendix Table~2 of the working paper reports the AC-B mean absolute deviation for rounds 1--3, 4--6, and 7--10 ($6.03$, $3.70$, $2.26$); their round-weighted mean, $\mathrm{MAD}=3.82$, gives the benchmark $5.46$, and the 95\% interval $[2.43, 8.49]$ corresponds to a standard error of $1.08$ on the MAD. The same table reports the shares of bids more than \$1 above and more than \$1 below value; their round-weighted means, $2\%$ and $49\%$, set the direction weights.

\paragraph{Kagel and Levin (1986) and Levin, Kagel, and Richard (1996): common values.}
Common-value studies do not report enough to reconstruct the bid-optimal benchmark, which depends on the posterior value conditional on winning. We therefore use a profit-based deviation, $\Delta^{\mathrm{CV}}=100\cdot|\bar\pi-\bar\pi^\star|/\bar\pi^\star$, where $\bar\pi$ is the reported mean realized profit and $\bar\pi^\star$ the reported risk-neutral predicted profit, pooling the small, medium, and large signal ranges. The first-price value comes from the sealed first-price auctions of \citet{kagel1986winner}, whose Table~2 reports average actual profits and average profits under the risk-neutral Nash equilibrium for each auction series; the second-price value comes from the English auctions of \citet{levin1996revenue}, whose Table~2 reports actual and theoretical average profits by signal range and number of bidders. The English auction is an open ascending format, so this comparison relies on its strategic similarity to the sealed second-price auction; the two values come from different studies. Intervals are obtained by drawing $\bar\pi$ and $\bar\pi^\star$ from normal distributions with the reported standard errors (100 draws). Because these benchmarks measure profits rather than bids, the common-value cells are compared only with each other and do not enter the private-value rank correlation.

Our comparisons concern orderings of deviations across formats rather than recall of specific numbers, and no source statistic appears in any prompt; this reduces, but does not rule out, the possibility that model behavior reflects exposure to this literature.

\paragraph{Mapping original reported statistics into the unified deviation metric.}

\textbf{(Li; Breitmoser--Schweighofer-Kodritsch) AC/AC-B/2P under APV.}
These papers report Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD)\footnote{For clock auctions, since the winner did not submit a final sealed bid, the reported MAD is computed only from bidders who revealed a bid by dropping out (i.e., those who exited the clock), excluding the winner's unobserved terminal willingness-to-pay.}
 from truthful bidding. In Li (2017), the reported deviation is based on a group-level statistic involving the second-highest bid and second-highest value; in Breitmoser--Schweighofer-Kodritsch, deviations are computed from submitted bids (often focusing on non-winning bids). In each case we adhere to the paper's unit of observation, but the SMAD definition conceptually applies to the underlying submitted bids.
Because the equilibrium optimum under AC and 2P is truthful bidding $b^\star(v)=v$, we have $d_m=|b-v|$ and thus
\[
\Delta_m = 100\cdot \frac{\mathrm{MAD}_m}{\mu_m^\star},
\qquad
\mu_m^\star=\mathbb E[v].
\]
Here $\mathbb E[v]$ is implied by the experimental value distribution reported by the paper (e.g., $\mathbb E[v]=70$ in Li; and $\mathbb E[v]=70$ in Breitmoser--Schweighofer-Kodritsch, whose value distribution is the same).\footnote{When auctions are repeated, we follow the paper's convention and report post-learning blocks (e.g., dropping the first three rounds and using the next reported block).}
If the paper reports $\mathrm{SE}(\mathrm{MAD}_m)$ (often using group means as the unit of observation), we propagate it via
\[
\mathrm{SE}(\widehat{\Delta}_m)=100\cdot \frac{\mathrm{SE}(\widehat{\mathrm{MAD}}_m)}{\widehat{\mu}_m^\star},
\quad
\mathrm{CI}_{95\%}(\Delta_m)=\widehat{\Delta}_m \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{SE}(\widehat{\Delta}_m).
\]

\textbf{(Kagel--Levin 1993) First-/Second-/Third-price IPV.}
Kagel--Levin report bid regressions and over-/under-bidding frequencies for fixed bidder counts (e.g., $N\in\{5,10\}$). We treat each $N$ separately: the regression form $\hat b(x)=\hat\alpha+\hat\beta x$ is fit for a \emph{fixed} number of bidders, and is not pooled across varying $N$.
Theory gives linear equilibrium bid functions under IPV with $x\sim U[x_{\min},x_{\max}]$:

$$\begin{aligned}
b^\star_{\mathrm{FP\_IPV}}(x) &= x - \frac{x-x_{\min}}{N} = \frac{N-1}{N}x + \frac{1}{N}x_{\min} \\
b^\star_{\mathrm{SP\_IPV}}(x) &= x \\
b^\star_{\mathrm{TP\_IPV}}(x) &= x + \frac{x-x_{\min}}{N-2} = \frac{N-1}{N-2}x - \frac{1}{N-2}x_{\min}
\end{aligned}$$

(When $x_{\min}=0$, these reduce to the familiar slope-only formulas.)
Because only summary moments are reported, we reconstruct the bid distribution via a parametric data-generating process (DGP) consistent with Table~2.
For each auction series and fixed $N$, we assume $x\sim U[x_{\min},x_{\max}]$ (bounds from the paper), and generate bids as
\[
b = \hat\alpha+\hat\beta x + \varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon\sim \mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2),
\]
where $(\mu,\sigma)$ are fitted by moment matching to the reported frequencies (e.g., $\Pr(b>x)$ and $\Pr(b>b^\star(x))$).
Given the fitted DGP, we estimate
\[
\widehat{\mathrm{MAD}}_m = \mathbb E\!\left[\,|b-b^\star(x)|\,\right],
\qquad
\widehat{\mu}_m^\star=\mathbb E[b^\star(x)],
\qquad
\widehat{\Delta}_m = 100\cdot \frac{\widehat{\mathrm{MAD}}_m}{\widehat{\mu}_m^\star}.
\]
In practice, we run the Monte Carlo procedure 100 times and report the across-run mean $\widehat{\Delta}_m$ as the point estimate; uncertainty is obtained from across-run variability (percentile or normal approximation).


\textbf{(Levin--Kagel--Richard 1996 and related CV papers) First-price CV and English (second-price proxy).}
In common-value auctions, bidders observe signals $s_i$ about a common value $V$ (e.g., $s_i=V+\varepsilon_i$ as specified in the original paper).

Theory gives linear equilibrium bid functions under Common Value:
$$\begin{aligned}
b^*_{FP\_CV}(x) &= x - \epsilon + Y\\
Y &= \left( \frac{2\epsilon}{N+1} \right) \exp\left[ -\left( \frac{N}{2\epsilon} \right) (x - (\underline{x} + \epsilon)) \right]\\
b^*_{SP\_CV}(x) &= x
\end{aligned}$$

The theoretical benchmark is the equilibrium bidding strategy $b_m^\star(s)$ implied by the paper's signal structure and mechanism. We therefore compute SMAD \emph{on bids} exactly as in private values:
\[
d_m = |b - b_m^\star(s)|,
\qquad
\Delta_m = 100\cdot \frac{\mathbb E[|b-b_m^\star(s)|]}{\widehat{\mu}_m^\star}.
\]

Because there is a very large common part in each players' valuation, we take the following normalization factor:
\[
{\widehat{\mu}_m^\star} = \mathbb E[b^\star_{max}(x) - b^\star_{min}(x)]
\]

When micro-data are unavailable, we reconstruct the bid distribution by simulating the paper's DGP (signals and value draws) together with the theoretical bidding rule $b_m^\star(\cdot)$, and fit any auxiliary noise parameters by moment matching to the summary statistics reported (e.g., frequencies of overbidding/high bids, or mean prices/profits as available). We run Monte Carlo 100 times and report the across-run mean $\widehat{\Delta}_m$ as the point estimate; uncertainty is obtained from across-run variability (percentile or normal approximation).

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Reconstruction-Uncertainty Bands}{Reconstruction-Uncertainty Bands}}\label{app:bands}

Beyond the Monte Carlo simulation noise at fixed mixture parameters, we represent uncertainty in the fitted parameters themselves by a parametric bootstrap over the mixture parameters. For each source--treatment pair we draw $B=2000$ vectors of $(p_{\text{eq}}, p_{\text{over}}, p_{\text{under}}, \lambda, \sigma, \delta)$ from admissible ranges pinned to the reported statistics and their granularity, propagate each draw through the reconstruction that defines the anchor, and report percentile bands. The point anchors are reproduced exactly at the central (published) parameter values before any perturbation, so the bands are centered on the numbers quoted in the main text. The admissible ranges are as follows. The direction weights $(p_{\text{eq}}, p_{\text{over}}, p_{\text{under}})$ are re-drawn from the sampling uncertainty of the reported over-/under-/at-value frequencies: a multinomial resample of the reported cell counts for \citet{kagel1993independent}, and a $\pm 5$ percentage-point range around each reported rate for \citet{li2017obviously} and \citet{breitmoser2022obviousness}, whose rates are stated only to that granularity (``${\sim}50\%$ dominant-strategy play'' admits $45$--$55\%$). The mean bid-to-value ratio is re-drawn through the reported bid-regression slope within its standard error ($\mathrm{SE}\approx 0.01$), which is the dominant driver of the sealed-bid IPV bands: for first-price the anchor is the bid-shading gap between human bids, which sit essentially at value, and the risk-neutral equilibrium $\tfrac{N-1}{N}v$. The equilibrium noise $\sigma$ is re-drawn through the reported $R^2$ within its reporting precision. The underbid bound $\delta$ is re-drawn over the range $[0.10, 0.25]$ used in the reconstruction. For the clock and APV formats the reported mean absolute deviation is re-drawn within its published standard error, which reproduces the confidence intervals carried in Table~\ref{tab:mixture_fit}. The overbid scale $\lambda$ governs the shape rather than the level of the deviation mass and is pinned so that the central mixture reproduces the published anchor.

These are reconstruction bands, not sampling bands on human behavior: they quantify how far the reconstructed anchor can move within what the source statistics leave undetermined. Table~\ref{tab:bands} reports them. Under the bands, the two human orderings our comparisons rely on are stable: the first-price $\gg$ second-price difficulty gap holds in $100\%$ of re-draws (SMAD ratio $95\%$ band $[3.54, 5.65]$, centered on the $4.4\times$ point gap), and the clock improvement over sealed second-price bidding holds in $100\%$ of re-draws for the open clock (AC) and in \rev{$96.5\%$} for the closed clock (AC-B), the latter's wider band inherited from the large reported standard error on the \citet{breitmoser2022obviousness} mean absolute deviation. Because the reconstructions carry this uncertainty, we use them only for orderings, directions, and comparative statics, never for level comparisons or hypothesis tests against LLM cells. The bootstrap is implemented in \texttt{analysis/reconstruction\_bands.py}.

\section{LLM Simulation Procedure}

We give the prompt-and-parse pipeline used to simulate various auctions with LLM agents. Each simulated bidder is queried independently. In every auction instance, the agent (i) receives the scenario and mechanism description, (ii) is asked to articulate a short bidding plan, and (iii) outputs a bid/decision in a structured format that can be parsed deterministically.


\subsection{Simulation process in sealed-bid auctions}\label{app:process}

\paragraph{Prompt structure.}
We attach the canonical prompt template in the following. Each bidder is assigned a role (e.g., ``Bidder Andy'') and informed of the other bidders' identities. The prompt then provides (a) a mechanism-specific \emph{rule explanation} (e.g., first-price vs.\ second-price vs.\ third-price), and (b) universal \emph{instructions} that define the objective and response format. We require the model to respond using two exact tags: \verb|<PLAN>| (a brief natural-language rationale) and \verb|<ACTION>| (the bid as a number) in the Chain-of-Thought style \citep{wei2022chain}:


\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{minipage}{0.95\textwidth}
\begin{lstlisting}
    You are Bidder Andy.
    You are bidding with {OTHER_BIDDERS}.

    {RULE EXPLANATION} + {INSTRUCTIONS}

    Your response must use these EXACT tags below:
    ```
    <PLAN>[ Reason out a bidding strategy and plan for this round. {% if current_round > 1 %}Learn from your previous rounds. Remember: the values will be different in future rounds - don't assume patterns will repeat. {% else %}Consider the history above if available. {% endif %}Be detailed and precise. LIMIT your plan to 100 words. ] </PLAN>
    ```

\subsection{Stylized eBay proxy-bidding environment}\label{app:ebay_env}
The eBay-style environment is separate from the laboratory benchmarks, so we report it here.
Because our eBay-style results section studies a richer, marketplace-inspired microstructure rather than canonical lab mechanisms, we treat eBay simulations as a separate environment. We implement a stylized eBay auction with \emph{proxy bidding} and an optional auction-extension closing rule. Proxy bidding means each bidder maintains a \emph{maximum bid} (their willingness-to-pay cap), and the platform automatically raises the standing bid only as needed to keep the current leader ahead. In each eBay simulation, there are $n=3$ bidders and (unless modified by the closing rule) the auction lasts for $T_{\text{eBay}}=10$ discrete bidding periods. Unless otherwise noted, the eBay environment uses IPV with $v_i \sim \mathrm{Unif}[0,99]$.

\emph{Treatments (closing rule).}
We vary one design dimension, the closing rule, yielding two treatments: (T1) the standard eBay auction with proxy bidding and a hard close, and (T2) the same auction with a modified closing rule (auction extension) in the spirit of early Amazon auctions.

\emph{Discrete-time implementation of bidding.}
Because LLM interactions are necessarily discrete, we approximate continuous-time bidding using period-based actions. In each period, bidders move in a common-knowledge, randomly permuted order. When it is a bidder's turn, they observe the current auction state (including updates induced by earlier movers in that period) and decide whether to \emph{increase their maximum bid} or \emph{hold}. In the final period, bidding is simultaneous: bidders submit their final decisions without observing others' last-period actions, capturing the idea that, in continuous time, a bidder cannot know whether another bid will arrive just before closing.

\emph{Modified closing rule (auction extension).}
Under the modified closing rule, if a higher maximum bid is placed in the final period such that a new bidder becomes the winner, the auction is extended by one additional period. This process repeats until a final period occurs in which no further bids are made.

\emph{Abstraction and prompting.}
This stylized eBay framework abstracts away from additional platform features such as shipping fees and Buy-It-Now options. Moreover, unlike the explicit framing interventions in Section~\ref{session:Intervention}, we keep the eBay prompts deliberately \emph{minimal} and descriptive: the goal is to study how LLMs behave under richer market microstructure without steering them toward any particular strategy through didactic instruction. The complete simulation process is described in Appendix~\ref{app:ebay}, and the corresponding prompts are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:prompt}.

\section{Examining Learning Effects in Repeated Games}\label{app:learning}

To assess whether LLM agents exhibited learning or adaptation across rounds, we conduct a set of statistical tests comparing early-round behavior to late-round behavior and examining continuous trends over time. For the First-Price Sealed-Bid (FPSB) auction, the mean SMAD in the first five rounds (0.2523) is essentially identical to that in the last five rounds (0.2524), with no detectable difference in either a t-test ($p = 0.9968$) or a Mann–Whitney U test ($p = 0.9131$). Trend analyses likewise show no evidence of systematic change: a linear regression yields a slope indistinguishable from zero (p = 0.9886) and a Spearman test finds no monotonic movement ($\rho = 0.0929$, $p = 0.7420$). Results are similar for the Second-Price Sealed-Bid (SPSB) auction. The comparison between the first and last five rounds reveals no significant difference (means 0.1282 vs. 0.1447; t-test $p = 0.3335$; Mann–Whitney $p = 0.1948$), and neither a linear trend test (p = 0.3664) nor a Spearman correlation test ($\rho= 0.3964$, $p = 0.1435$) indicates meaningful temporal structure. Taken together, across all specifications, we find no statistical evidence of learning effects, behavioral drift, or systematic changes in bidding deviations over the 15 rounds. LLM agents' deviation magnitudes remain stable throughout both FPSB and SPSB environments.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Winner's Curse: Details by Auction Format and Number of Bidders}{Winner's Curse: Details by Auction Format and Number of Bidders}}\label{app:winners_curse}

This appendix collects the GPT-4o common-value results summarized in Section~\ref{sec:tier_orderings}, following the design of \citet{kagel1986winner}.

Common-value auctions provide a particularly sharp test of whether bidders correctly condition on the adverse selection implicit in winning. In the canonical experimental design of \citet{kagel1986winner}, each bidder observes a noisy private signal $x$ about a common underlying value $x_0$. Winning is therefore ``bad news'': conditional on having the highest signal among $n$ bidders, the expected common value is strictly below $x$, with the gap widening in $n$.\footnote{In the standard symmetric-information design, $\mathbb{E}[x_0 \mid X=x_{1:n}] = x - \frac{n-1}{n+1}\varepsilon$ for signals away from boundary regions, where $\varepsilon$ controls signal noise. This is why competition worsens adverse selection: as $n$ rises, the winner must make a larger downward correction.} If bidders neglect (or incompletely correct for) this conditioning, they overbid relative to $\mathbb{E}[x_0\mid \text{win}]$ and the winner earns negative profits: the classic \emph{winner's curse}. Laboratory evidence documents both comparative statics emphasized in our setting: (i) winner losses become larger as the number of bidders increases, and (ii) institutions that reveal more information during the price discovery process attenuate the curse relative to sealed-bid first-price formats. For example, \citet{kagel1986winner} reports substantial negative profits conditional on winning that are markedly worse with more bidders (e.g., approximately $-\$1.68$ with $n=4$ versus $-\$3.68$ with $n=7$ in their benchmark conditions), and related work shows that the revenue advantage of ascending formats hinges on whether bidders have learned to avoid severe winner's-curse mistakes \citep{levin1996revenue}.

We evaluate whether LLM bidders exhibit analogous winner's-curse patterns in common-value \emph{first-price sealed-bid} (FPSB) auctions. Table~\ref{tab:winner_curse_llm} summarizes \emph{winner profits} in our simulations as we vary the number of bidders from 4 to 7 (holding the environment fixed). A clear winner’s-curse pattern emerges: winners lose money on average in every FPSB setting, and losses grow with competition (mean winner profit: $-2.54$ with 4 bidders, $-4.40$ with 5 bidders, $-5.46$ with 6 bidders, and $-5.55$ with 7 bidders). Consistent with this monotonic decline, the share of winners earning positive profits collapses from 24\% (4 bidders) to 0\% (7 bidders). One-sided tests strongly reject the hypothesis that winner profit is nonnegative in all FPSB treatments, confirming that LLM bidders fall prey to the winner’s curse\citep{kagel1986winner}.

To benchmark the role of auction format, we also simulate a common-value \emph{second-price sealed-bid} (SPSB) environment under the same information structure. The same comparative-static forces appear, but the curse is attenuated: with 4 bidders, winners earn positive profits on average (mean $=1.30$), profits are near zero with 5 bidders, and winner losses become statistically significant only once competition is sufficiently intense ($n\ge 6$). Thus, while both formats exhibit adverse-selection pressures that grow in $n$, LLM behavior displays a substantially stronger winner’s curse in FPSB than in SPSB, mirroring \rev{the human finding that the curse is stronger in first-price sealed-bid auctions than in English auctions \citep{kagel1986winner, levin1996revenue}}.

\subsection{Obviously Strategy-Proof Prompts}\label{sec:osp-prompts}
The sealed-bid prompt follows the style of the last section's prompts closely. The only difference is in the first two paragraphs, where agents are informed of the APV environment. For the SPSB, we describe the auction format as follows:
\begin{figure}
\centering
\begin{minipage}{0.95\textwidth}
\begin{lstlisting}
In this game, you will participate in an auction for a prize against {{num_bidders}} other bidders. You will play this game for {{n}} rounds.
At the start of each round, bidders will see their value for the prize. Your value for the prize will be calculated as follows:
1. First we will randomly draw a common value between {{common_low}} and {{common_high}}, with all values equally likely.
2. Then, for each bidder, a private taste adjustment will be drawn between 0 and {{private}}, with all values equally likely.
Your value for the prize is equal to the common value plus your private taste adjustment. You will not learn the common value or your private taste adjustment separately. This means that each person in your group may have a different value for the prize. However, if you have a high value, it is more likely that other people in your group have a high value.
After learning your value, you will submit a bid privately at the same time as the other bidders. Bids must be in ${{increment}} increments.
The highest bidder wins the prize and pays the second-highest bid. If you win, your earnings will increase by your value for the prize, and decrease by the second-highest bid. If you don't win, your earnings will remain unchanged.
After each auction, we will display all bids. Ties for the highest bid will be resolved randomly.
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}
\label{fig:sealbid_prompt-4}
\end{figure}

The implementation of the clock auction simulates a global clock price, with prices beginning at $0$ and increasing in $\$1$ increments. The clock auction format descriptions closely mirror the SPSB format above, but with the added description of the clock mechanism in the fourth and fifth paragraphs.

Your value for the prize is equal to the common value plus your private taste adjustment. You will not learn the common value or your private taste adjustment separately. This means that each person in your group may have a different value for the prize. However, if you have a high value, it is more likely that other people in your group have a high value.
The auction proceeds as follows: First, you will learn your value for the prize. Then, the auction will start. We will display a price to everyone in your group that starts at 0 and counts upwards in {{increment}} USD increments, up to a maximum of {{common_high + private}}. At any point, you can choose to leave the auction, and anytime a bidder leaves, we will broadcast that information to all the remaining bidders.
When there is only one bidder left in the auction, that bidder will win the prize at the current price. If you win, your earnings will increase by your value for the prize, and decrease by the current price. If you don't win, your earnings will remain unchanged.
After each auction, we will display all bids. Ties for the highest bid will be resolved randomly.
\end{lstlisting}

The AC-B auction description closely resembles the AC format, but with the explicit caveat that we will not notify bidders when competitors drop out.


\subsection{Common Value Auctions}

For the Second-Price Sealed-Bid auction in the Common Value setting, the rule is

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{minipage}{0.95\textwidth}
\begin{lstlisting}
In this game, you will participate in an auction for a prize against {{num_bidders}} other bidders. You will play this game for {{n}} rounds.
At the start of each round, bidders will see their perceived value for the prize- a noisy measurement of the true value of the prize. Your perceived value for the prize will be calculated as follows:
1. For each round, a common value will be drawn between {{common_low}} and {{common_high}}, with all values equally likely to be drawn.
2. For each person, a private noisy adjustment will be drawn between -{{private}} and {{private}}, with all values equally likely to be drawn.
We will tell you your perceived value, the sum of the common value and the private noise adjustment. However, everyone's true value for the prize is equal to the shared common value.
After learning your perceived value, you will submit a bid privately at the same time as the other bidders. Bids must be in ${{increment}} increments.
The highest bidder wins the prize and pays the second-highest bid. If you win, your earnings will increase by the true value for the prize, and decrease by the second-highest bid. If you don't win, your earnings will remain unchanged.
After each auction, we will display all bids. Ties for the highest bid will be resolved randomly.
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}
\label{fig:sealbid_prompt-6}
\end{figure}


\subsection{Ebay-proxy prompt}
For the Standard eBay auction with proxy bidding (T1), the rule is:
\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{minipage}{0.95\textwidth}
\begin{lstlisting}
In this game, you will participate in an eBay auction for an item against {{num_bidders}} other bidders. This auction will last for {{num_rounds}} days. All dollar amounts in this game are in US Dollars ($).

Item Details:
Item Description: {{item_description}}
Item Condition: {{item_condition}}
Your Private Value: At the start of each round, bidders will see their value for the item, randomly drawn between $0 and ${{private}}, with all values equally likely. After learning your value for the item, you will submit a maximum bid. Bids must be in ${{increment}} increments.

Auction Format:
This is an eBay auction. The auction starts at ${{start_price}} and will last for {{num_rounds}} days. eBay uses "proxy bidding." This means that if you wish to enter the auction, you should submit your maximum bid, and eBay will automatically bid on your behalf, up to your maximum, only as much as necessary to maintain your position as the highest bidder. Each day you will see the current price and have the opportunity to increase your maximum bid. If you do not want to increase your maximum bid, then output HOLD.

You may place bids at any point during the auction, even on the final day. No bidder will know if they (or anyone else) is the last bidder on the final day.

The highest bidder wins the prize and pays the auction price at the time the auction's clock expired. If you win, your earnings will increase by your value for the prize, and decrease by the final auction price. If you don't win, your earnings will remain unchanged. Ties for the highest bid will be resolved randomly.
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}

\end{figure}

\subsection{Intervention Prompt}\label{app:intervention}

We designed the clock framing intervention prompt based on \citet{breitmoser2022obviousness}.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{minipage}{0.95\textwidth}
\begin{lstlisting}
FIRST STAGE: Sealed Bid
    You will submit a sealed bid privately at the same time as the other bidders. This bid will serve as your automatic exit price in the next stage.
SECOND STAGE: Ascending Clock (Simulation)
    After the sealed bid stage, we will simulate an ascending clock auction.
    The clock will start at 0 and increase in increments of {increment}.
    The clock will display the current price. You will also see that there are a total of \{num$\_$bidders\} bidders participating, although you do not know other bidder's values.
    If the current price on the clock reaches or exceeds your sealed bid from the first stage, you will automatically exit the auction. The auction ends when only one bidder is left remaining in the second stage based on their bid from the first stage.
    END OF AUCTION
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}
\label{fig:sealbid_prompt-12}
\end{figure}
